Two Christians can sit at the same table and order very different things, and both can be wrong.

One is careless — the body is temporary, so what does it matter. The other is terrified — the body is a project, so every mouthful is a moral exam. Scripture rejects both, and it does so with one image.

"What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?" (1 Corinthians 6:19).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **your body is not your private property to spend as you please; it is a temple on loan, and stewardship of it is worked out in the unglamorous daily acts of eating, resting, and ruling your appetites.**

## The Owner and the Steward

### "Ye Are Not Your Own"

That phrase is the whole argument, and it is uncomfortable in the best way.

Paul does not say your body is valuable. He says it is *not yours*. There is an owner, and the owner paid: "For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20).

A steward is not the owner of the estate. He is the trusted manager who will give an account. That is your relationship to your body, and it changes the questions you ask about it.

**Kingdom Stewardship** does not begin with "what do I feel like doing with my body?" It begins with "what has the Owner asked of me here?"

### The Temple That Is a Person

A temple is not a warehouse; it is a meeting place. That is the point of the image.

Under the old covenant, the temple was where God's presence dwelt among His people. Under the new, the dwelling place is a body. Which is why Paul can say the body is the temple of the Spirit *in you*.

Those last two words matter: **the Spirit is already resident.** Stewardship is not inviting Him in; it is not driving Him out.

### Not a Religion of Rules

Here is where stewardship gets twisted. Some teaching turns the temple into a dietary law with an extra helping of shame.

Paul had already fought that battle. In the same letter he insists, "meats for the belly, and the belly for meats: but God shall destroy both it and them" (1 Corinthians 6:13) — food is not the centre of the moral universe.

And Romans 14 gives the governing principle: "Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth" (Romans 14:3).

**Stewardship is a relationship with a Person, not a performance for an audience.**

### The One Ownership That Changes Appetite

There is a subtle gift inside the "bought with a price" logic. If I am not my own, then my appetite is not my master either.

Paul says it plainly elsewhere: "All things are lawful unto me, but all things are not expedient: all things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any" (1 Corinthians 6:12).

That last clause is the test: *brought under the power of any*. Not "is this allowed," but "does this rule me?"

### The Three Daily Acts

#### Eat: Nourishment, Not Ruling

Food is a gift with a purpose, and the purpose is strength, not mastery.

The Old Testament picture is instructive. Daniel and his friends refused the king's table — not because food was evil but because it was attached to an idolatrous system — and asked for pulse and water. At the end, "their countenances appeared fairer and fatter in flesh than all the children which did eat the portion of the king's meat" (Daniel 1:15).

The lesson is not a diet plan. It is that **a person who can say no to a table is a person whose body is not running the house.**

Proverbs gives the same wisdom in a single line: "Hast thou found honey? eat so much as is sufficient for thee, lest thou be filled therewith, and vomit it" (Proverbs 25:16). Sufficiency, not prohibition.

#### Rest: The Act Nobody Counts

Rest is the stewardship nobody praises, because it looks like nothing and produces no visible achievement.

Yet God built it into the week before the Fall. And Jesus offered it as a load-bearing invitation: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

Rest is not laziness and it is not luxury. It is the acknowledgment that the temple has a Keeper who does not sleep — and the steward who never rests is behaving like the owner.

#### Rule: The Spirit Over the Appetite

The third act is the daily exercise of self-government.

Paul's athletic metaphor is unflinching: "But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway" (1 Corinthians 9:27).

*Keep under* is *hypōpiazō* — to strike under the eye, to subdue. It is a strong word, and it is aimed at the part of us that wants to sit on the throne.

But note what this is not: it is not hatred of the body. You do not train a horse you despise. **You subdue what you intend to ride into battle.**

## What This Means for You

**1. Ask the ownership question.** Before the next decision that touches your body, ask what the Owner would have you do with what is His.

**2. Practise sufficiency at one meal.** Not fasting, not a rule — simply eating enough and stopping.

**3. Put rest on the calendar like a meeting.** If it is not scheduled, it will not happen.

**4. Find your "under the power" item.** Ask honestly what rules you, and bring that one thing to God.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** Stewardship includes using the care God has provided. A steward who refuses the doctor is not being more spiritual; he is being less wise.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 1 Corinthians 6:12–20** daily, and mark the word "body."
- **Read Daniel 1:8–16** and note Daniel's manner, not only his menu.
- **Schedule one full rest day** this week and protect it.
- **Read 1 Corinthians 9:24–27** and mark the athletic verbs.
- **Read Proverbs 25:16, 28** and write out the connection between appetite and walls.
