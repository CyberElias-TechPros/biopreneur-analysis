Jesus chose a strange word for healing, and He chose it in a conversation that has offended readers for twenty centuries.

"It is not meet to take the children's bread, and to cast it to dogs" (Matthew 15:26).

He did not call healing a reward, a prize, or a miracle granted to the deserving. He called it **bread**.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **healing is not a rare medicine administered to strangers but ordinary food set on the table for children, and the only question left is whether you know whose table you are sitting at.**

## The Word He Chose

### Bread, Not Medicine

Start with the category, because the category decides the posture.

Medicine is scarce, administered by permission, and rationed to the urgent. Bread is none of those things. Bread is ordinary, expected, daily, and given without a negotiation.

That is the word the Lord used for healing. And He used it in a sentence about children.

**You do not petition a father for bread the way you petition a stranger for medicine.** The first is a household matter; the second is an application.

So which of the two have you been doing?

### What "Meet" Means

"It is not meet" is *ouk estin kalon* — it is not good, not fitting, not proper. The same word family as "beautiful."

So Jesus is not saying the woman is unworthy. He is saying the arrangement is wrong — the order of the household does not permit the children's food to be handed out the door.

That distinction matters enormously, because it means the sentence is about **priority**, not about **worth**. And a woman who heard only the second half of it would have gone home.

### The Children First

Mark's version adds a word that clarifies everything: "Let the children first be filled" (Mark 7:27).

*First.* Not only. The word contains an order, not an exclusion — and the end of the gospel is the proof. The same Jesus who said "first" also said, "Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature" (Mark 16:15).

So the sentence that sounds like a locked door is in fact a sentence with a timetable in it. The children's bread is not the only bread. It is the bread that is already on the table.

### Who the Children Are

Which brings the reader in, and this is the turn of the whole article.

In Matthew 15, "the children" meant Israel — the household to whom the promises were first given. You and I were not born into that household.

And that is precisely why the rest of the New Testament matters so much here. Paul writes: "That the Gentiles should be fellowheirs, and of the same body, and partakers of his promise in Christ by the gospel" (Ephesians 3:6).

**Fellowheirs.** Not guests at the table. Not dogs under it. Heirs.

The woman in Matthew 15 was asking for crumbs from a table she had no right to. You are not in that position. **You are in the position of the child whose name is on the place card.**

### "Truth, Lord"

And yet her answer is the greatest thing in the passage, and it is why Jesus called her faith great — one of only two people in the Gospels to receive that commendation.

"Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table" (Matthew 15:27).

Read what she did. She did not argue with His character. She did not dispute His priority. She agreed with the sentence — and then found a promise inside it.

She reasoned from the master to the table: if the dogs are fed, it is because the master feeds them. She did not challenge the order; she simply refused to believe the master of the house would let anyone in it starve.

And Jesus answered, "O woman, great is thy faith: be it unto thee even as thou wilt" (Matthew 15:28). And her daughter was made whole from that very hour.

### Great Faith and Great Noise

It is worth noticing what Jesus did *not* call great faith in the Gospels, because we have a habit of assuming it.

He did not call great faith the loudest prayer. He did not call great faith the longest fast. In Matthew 8, a centurion said, "speak the word only, and my servant shall be healed" (Matthew 8:8), and Jesus marvelled: "I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel" (Matthew 8:10).

Two foreigners. Two sentences. Both about **the authority and the willingness** of the One being asked, not about the intensity of the asker.

**The faith that moves Jesus is not the faith that shouts. It is the faith that has correctly estimated the household.**

### The Bread and the Body

And there is a final turn in the metaphor that the New Testament will not let us miss.

Jesus took bread at the Last Supper and said, "This is my body, which is given for you" (Luke 22:19). And the bread of healing is tied to the body given — Isaiah's suffering servant, of whom Matthew says plainly: "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" (Matthew 8:17, quoting Isaiah 53:4).

So the bread on the table is not generic goodwill. **It was bought.** Your **Inheritance of the Believer** is not an allowance granted at the Father's discretion; it is a distribution of what was already purchased.

### The Table and the Orphan

One illustration, because the difference is easier to feel than to define.

Picture two children in the same house. One comes to the table at mealtime, sits down, and reaches for the bread. The other stands at the doorway asking whether he might possibly be allowed a piece, and is still asking when the meal is cleared away.

Same house. Same bread. Same father.

**The difference is not favour; it is whether the child knows he is a child.** That is what **Sonship** means in practice, and it is why an orphan spirit makes a believer sicker than any virus — it keeps him negotiating at a table where his place has already been laid.

### Some Honest Guardrails

Three things must be said, because this teaching is easily turned into a club.

**First, healing on a schedule.** Some believers are healed instantly; some gradually; some wait. Luke 17 records ten lepers cleansed "as they went" (Luke 17:14) — the healing was in the walking, not before it. The bread is yours; the eating is not always at the hour you chose.

**Second, a word to the sick.** If you are unwell as you read this, the verse is not a verdict on your faith. Nothing in Matthew 15 says the woman's daughter had been ill because of unbelief. The article is written to give you a table to sit at, not a failure to confess.

**Third, bread and wisdom.** Faith and wisdom work together: the same Father who provides healing also provided the doctor, the medicine, the rest, and the friend who drives you to the appointment. Refusing all of it while demanding a crumb is not faith. It is a misunderstanding of how a household is run.

## What This Means for You

**1. Change the category you pray from.** Not "will He?" but "it is bread, and I am at the table." That single shift turns a plea into a meal.

**2. Settle whose child you are before you ask.** **Divine Wholeness** is the inheritance of a son, not a reward for a servant. You do not qualify for it; you receive it.

**3. Say "Truth, Lord" to whatever He has said.** The woman's greatness was in agreeing with the hard sentence and finding the promise in it — not in arguing her case.

**4. Notice the word "first."** If your healing has been delayed, "first" is not "never." Stand where she stood and keep standing.

**5. Take the bread and the ordinary means together.** Ask for healing, and keep the appointment. Both come from the same table.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 15:21–28** and Mark 7:24–30 side by side, and mark the word "first."
- **Write down one symptom** and beside it write "children's bread."
- **Read Isaiah 53:4–5**, and notice what Matthew says it means.
- **Eat one meal at the table** with your Father, not at the gate.
