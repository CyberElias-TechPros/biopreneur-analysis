There is a kind of talk about the body that is so ordinary nobody notices they are doing it, and Proverbs says it is a matter of life and death.

"Death and life are in the power of the tongue: and they that love it shall eat the fruit thereof" (Proverbs 18:21).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **your words about your body are not neutral commentary on a situation; they are seeds that set a harvest in motion, and the tongue is the only organ in the body that can plant for the whole of it.**

## What the Proverb Claims

### "In the Power"

The Hebrew is *bəyad lāšôn* — literally "in the hand of the tongue." The hand is the instrument of taking and holding and doing. To be in the hand of something is to be under its control.

So the claim is not poetic decoration. **The tongue has been given a jurisdiction**, and what passes through it comes back as fruit.

And note the word "love." "They that love it shall eat the fruit thereof." That is an odd word for speech — until you notice how much people enjoy talking about their ailments. There is a pleasure in the telling, and Proverbs says the pleasure is not free.

### The Fruit

The verb is *yōʾḵal* — shall eat. The metaphor is agricultural and unavoidable. Words are not merely sounds that dissipate; they are seeds that produce a crop, and the speaker is the one who eats it.

That is worth sitting with, because it means the harvest is not primarily social. It is personal. The man who says "I'm always ill" is not mainly shaping other people's opinions. He is shaping his own expectations, and his expectations shape his conduct, and his conduct shapes his body.

### "The Tongue of the Wise Is Health"

And Proverbs supplies the positive counterpart, with a word that belongs to this month.

"There is that speaketh like the piercings of a sword: but the tongue of the wise is health" (Proverbs 12:18).

The word is *marpēʾ* — healing, a remedy, from *rāp̄āʾ*, the same root as the healer's name. So the wise tongue is not merely "nice." It is **medicinal**.

Set the two halves of the verse side by side, because the contrast is the teaching. Some speech pierces like a sword. Some speech heals like a remedy. Both are available every hour, and only one of them is wise.

### James and the Small Fire

James takes the same idea and makes it a doctrine of the whole person.

"Even so the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things. Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!" (James 3:5).

Then: "And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity... it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell" (James 3:6).

Look at what the tongue does in that verse: it defiles **the whole body**. Not the reputation, not the relationship — the body. And it sets on fire "the course of nature," *ton trochon tēs geneseōs*, the wheel of existence.

That is a much larger claim than "words hurt feelings." James is saying that the tongue is the ignition point of the whole life, and the fire travels.

### Jesus on the Overflow

Jesus gives the mechanism, and He puts the heart upstream of the mouth.

"Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh" (Matthew 12:34).

Then: "A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things" (Matthew 12:35).

So speech is a report, and it is also a deposit. What comes out of your mouth tells you what is stored in your heart, and what you keep saying reinforces what is stored. That loop is the reason the tongue matters so much; it is both the output and the input.

And Jesus adds the sentence that connects this to the whole month: "For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned" (Matthew 12:37).

### Faith and the Mountain

And there is a passage that has been badly handled on this subject, so let us handle it here.

"Whosoever shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed... and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have whatsoever he saith" (Mark 11:23).

Two observations. First, the emphasis in that verse falls on what is in the heart, not on the volume of the speech — "shall not doubt in his heart." The saying is effective because of the believing, not the other way round.

Second, Jesus said it to men who had just been impressed by a withered fig tree. He was teaching about a life of faith, not selling a technique for reciting wishes.

What it does establish: what you say and what you believe are connected, and the connection runs through the heart.

### What Speaking Health Is Not

Three corrections, because this teaching has a bad reputation and it has earned some of it.

**Not lying about symptoms.** Speaking health does not mean standing in front of a doctor and reporting a body you do not have. Jesus did not tell the lepers they were clean before they were cleansed; He told them to go and show themselves to the priest — and "as they went, they were cleansed" (Luke 17:14).

**Not the whole of confession.** **Confession of the Word** is *homologia*, saying the same thing God says. If God has not said it, saying it louder does not make it so. A confession that is not anchored in Scripture is optimism with better marketing.

**Not a substitute for means.** Declaring health while refusing the means is not what this month has been teaching. Faith and wisdom work together: speak the truth, take the medicine, and expect the Lord.

### The Chart and the Comment

One illustration, and it is a common one because it is accurate.

Consider the difference between two ways of talking about a diagnosis.

A man says: "My body is falling apart. It's always something. I've never been healthy."

Or he says: "This is a serious diagnosis. Here is what it is, here is what we are doing, and here is what I am trusting God for."

The first sentence is a verdict. The second is a report. Both are about the same diagnosis. Only one of them is speaking the truth that has a future in it.

Set them side by side and you can feel the difference in the body: one makes the shoulders drop, and the other produces a plan.

Which of the two have you been saying about yours?

And who taught you the first one?

### What to Say Instead

Practical, and it needs to be practical because "don't be negative" is useless advice.

**Say true and say promised.** Both. "My body has a problem, and by His stripes I was healed." That is not a contradiction; it is a whole account.

**Start with the smaller true thing.** If you cannot say "I am healed" with any conviction yet, say "He is faithful, and I am not doing this alone." That is not a lesser confession; it is a step.

**Say what you are doing.** "I am seeing the doctor on Thursday" is a statement of faith in a means, and it counts.

**And say it over others.** "The tongue of the wise is health" is not only about how you speak about yourself. The words you say over a sick friend are carrying something too.

## What This Means for You

**1. Listen to yourself this week.** Speak no differently, and just record. The report will surprise you.

**2. Find the verdict sentences.** Most people have three or four that they repeat about their body without thinking. Write yours down.

**3. Report, then promise.** That is a full, honest sentence, and it is available immediately.

**4. Set a watch on your mouth.** That is David's own prayer: "Set a watch, O LORD, before my mouth; keep the door of my lips" (Psalm 141:3). He asked for a guard, not a gag.

**5. Let your body be the reason.** You are not doing this to perform spirituality. You are doing it because **Divine Wholeness** is a matter of what you have been saying about it, and the harvest is yours to eat.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Proverbs 12 and 18**
- **Read James 3:1–12** slowly, and count the images.
- **Rewrite your three verdicts**
- **Say one of them aloud**
