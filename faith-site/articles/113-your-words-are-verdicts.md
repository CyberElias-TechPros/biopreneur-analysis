A judge does not describe what happened. That is the barrister's work. A judge decides what it means and what follows, and once the words are spoken, the room reorganises around them.

Scripture treats the human tongue as having something of that character: "Death and life are in the power of the tongue: and they that love it shall eat the fruit thereof" (Proverbs 18:21).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the tongue does not merely report reality; it pronounces on it — and a believer who understands that will treat speech as a verdict rather than as commentary.**

## The Word Behind the Proverb

The Hebrew word translated "power" here is not the usual word for strength. It carries the sense of a hand — the hand that holds, the hand that acts. The tongue is described as having a hand in the outcome. It is not merely a messenger; it is a participant.

That is stronger than the way we usually say it. "Words matter," we tell children, and they nod and go on speaking carelessly. Proverbs says something more precise: the tongue has a hand in the outcome of a life, and the person who loves to use it will eat what it produces.

The second half of the verse is the part that makes it personal: "they that love it shall eat the fruit thereof." Not "they that use it." They that *love* it. There is a category of person who enjoys the power of speech — the cutting remark, the decisive pronouncement, the satisfying summary of someone else's failure — and the proverb says that appetite has a harvest.

**Confession of the Word** — *homologia*, saying the same thing God says — is the disciplined use of this faculty. **The Authority of the Believer** — the right to stand in His name — is what gives your words weight when they are aligned with His. **The Word of God** — His living address — is the standard your verdicts must match.

Consider a clerk stamping documents. The stamp makes the document official, and it will make anything official that is placed under it, whether or not the document deserves it. That is precisely why the clerk is trained, supervised, and answerable.

### Why Speech Shapes the Speaker First

Most teaching on this subject rushes to talk about what words do to circumstances. Proverbs begins somewhere else: what words do to the one speaking them.

"You are what you say in private" is not in the text, but something like it is. A man who describes himself constantly as tired, finished, unlucky, and overlooked is not neutral about his own future. He is building a case, and he is the first person in the courtroom listening to it.

The psychological reality is not superstition. What you say out loud you rehearse; what you rehearse, you reinforce; what you reinforce becomes the frame you interpret the next week through. James says the tongue "setteth on fire the course of nature" (James 3:6) — the entire wheel of a person's life.

So the first question about a careless word is not "what did it do to the world?" but "what did it do to the speaker?"

### The Verdicts We Pronounce on Other People

There is a harder edge to this that gets left out of most sermons on the tongue.

Jesus says we will give account for "every idle word" (Matthew 12:36). Not every cruel word — every idle one. And His explanation connects speech to *being*: "for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh" (Matthew 12:34).

Every time you say "he's useless," "she's hopeless," "they'll never change," you have pronounced a verdict about a person's future that God has not authorised. You may be accurate about the present. You have still overstepped.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. Commentary says, "This is how he is right now." Verdict says, "This is what he will always be." One is an observation, which may be legitimate. The other is a sentence handed down on a life you do not hold. The believer is not authorised to issue the second, even in jest, and certainly not in anger.

### The Verdicts We Pronounce on Ourselves

The same overreach happens inward, and it is often dressed up as humility.

"I've always been this way." "I'll never be free of it." "That's just who I am." These are verdicts. They sound like honesty and are actually sentences of imprisonment passed on the basis of evidence from the past.

Paul writes, "I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" (Philippians 4:13) — a verdict. He had plenty of evidence to the contrary, including prison. He chose the verdict because he had received a different one from the Judge.

That is the whole discipline of Confession of the Word. It is not a denial of facts; it is a decision about what the facts mean, made on the authority of someone with jurisdiction.

### How to Make Your Mouth Count

This teaching goes wrong when it becomes either a formula or a silence. The Bible gives neither.

It does not give a formula, because a Christian who tries to speak his way to a better life without changing anything else will find that his tongue has become a source of self-deception rather than authority. And it does not give silence, because Scripture is full of people who spoke the truth aloud in the face of the evidence and watched God honour it.

So here is the discipline, and it is unglamorous.

First, get the verdict right. Before you speak about a situation, ask what God has said about it. If He has not said anything specific, speak about His character rather than about your preferred outcome.

Second, get the words precise. A specific verdict is stronger than a general one. "God is good" is true. "God said this, and I am standing on it" is a verdict that connects to a text.

Third, get the timing right. Not every thought needs to be spoken and not every situation needs comment. Ecclesiastes says to let your words be few (Ecclesiastes 5:2); a mouth that says less but means more is the one that carries weight.

Fourth, get the room right. Some verdicts belong spoken into your own heart, some into the presence of God, some into the gathered church, and some are meant to be said directly into the situation. Wisdom knows which one this is.

## What This Means for You

**1. Audit your private speech for one week.** What do you say about yourself, your family, your work, and your future when nobody is listening to you? That is your real theology of the tongue.

**2. Retire one sentence that has been functioning as a verdict.** Something like "I'm always like this" or "nothing ever works for me." Replace it with what the Word says, and say it enough for the new one to have a hearing.

**3. Refrain from pronouncing over people.** You may describe what someone did. You may not sentence them. This one change would alter many families and several churches.

**4. Speak in agreement with the text.** Keep a specific verse where you can see it. When the subject comes up, say what it says rather than what it feels like.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom make your words credible.** Faith speaks the verdict; wisdom makes your life agree with it. A man who declares that God has made him honest while continuing to deceive will find that his verdicts carry no weight even to his own ears. Faith and wisdom work together, and their agreement is what makes the mouth worth listening to.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Proverbs 18:20–21 and Matthew 12:33–37 together**, and note the connection both make between mouth and heart.
- **Write down three verdicts you have spoken recently** about yourself or someone else, and rewrite them as they should be.
- **Pray Psalm 141:3 each morning**: "Set a watch, O LORD, before my mouth."
- **Say the rewritten verdict out loud daily** for the rest of the week.
