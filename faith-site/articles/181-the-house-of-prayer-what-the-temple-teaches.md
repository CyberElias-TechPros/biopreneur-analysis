Of all the things God could have named His house for, He named it for one activity — and then attached a clause that offended the people who first heard it.

"For mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people" (Isaiah 56:7).

Not a house of sacrifice, though sacrifice happened there. Not a house of teaching, though teaching happened there. A house of prayer.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **God named His house for one activity and opened that name to all people, which means the measure of a house of prayer is not its architecture but whether the praying in it is available to everyone.**

## What the Name Means

### The Context That Makes It Shocking

Isaiah 56 is addressed to two categories of people who had been formally excluded from the assembly under the old covenant: eunuchs, and foreigners.

Deuteronomy 23 had barred both. A eunuch was not to enter the congregation (Deuteronomy 23:1), and an Ammonite or Moabite was excluded to the tenth generation (Deuteronomy 23:3).

And then God says this: "Neither let the son of the stranger, that hath joined himself to the LORD, speak, saying, The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people; neither let the eunuch say, Behold, I am a dry tree" (Isaiah 56:3).

And He answers both, by name, with a welcome — and then names the house for prayer "for all people."

**The name is a promise to the people who had been told they could not come.** That is not decoration; that is the whole point of the verse.

### Why Prayer and Not Sacrifice

It is worth asking why God chose prayer as the name, because the temple was, functionally, a slaughterhouse. Sacrifice was its daily business.

The answer is in who was being welcomed. A foreigner could bring a sacrifice only through the mediation of a priest and the machinery of the cult. But anybody can pray. **Prayer is the one service of the house that the outsider can perform as readily as the priest.**

So the name is a declaration of access. By calling it a house of prayer, God made the central activity of His house the one that has no barrier in it.

### Solomon Prayed This at the Dedication

And here is the detail that shows Isaiah 56:7 was not an afterthought.

At the dedication of the temple, Solomon prayed a long prayer, and a substantial portion of it was for the stranger.

"Moreover concerning a stranger, that is not of thy people Israel, but cometh out of a far country for thy name's sake... Hear thou in heaven thy dwelling place, and do according to all that the stranger calleth to thee for" (1 Kings 8:41–43).

Read his reason: "that all people of the earth may know thy name, to fear thee, as do thy people Israel."

**The house was dedicated with a prayer for outsiders.** The building was opened with a request that its door would work for people who had no claim on it.

Which means when the temple later became a place where the outsider's area was used as a shortcut, it was not a minor administrative failing. It was a contradiction of its own dedication.

### "A Den of Thieves"

Jesus quoted Isaiah 56:7 when He cleansed the temple, and He quoted a second verse with it.

"It is written, My house shall be called the house of prayer; but ye have made it a den of thieves" (Matthew 21:13).

The second phrase is from Jeremiah 7:11 — "Is this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?" — and in Jeremiah's context, the problem was a people who treated the temple as cover for conduct they had no intention of changing.

So Jesus is not merely objecting to inflated prices in the money-changing stalls. He is indicting a house that had been repurposed: **a place named for prayer had become a place that made prayer inconvenient for the people it was named to include.**

And note the detail Mark gives: He "would not suffer that any man should carry any vessel through the temple" (Mark 11:16). The court of the Gentiles was being used as a thoroughfare. The one space the outsider had was a corridor for other people's business.

### What the Traffic Was

Which raises the question this article must ask its reader.

The temple's problem was not that the traffic was wicked. Money-changing and animal-selling were necessary services for pilgrims. Somebody had to sell the doves.

The problem was that the necessary traffic had been allowed to occupy the space set apart for something else, until the something else could no longer happen there.

**Most of us do not have a temple to defile. We have a life. And the traffic in it is rarely evil — it is usually necessary, and it is usually louder than prayer.**

Ask it honestly, because the answer will be specific. What has taken the space? It will not be a scandal. It will be a phone, a schedule, a worry, a service to others that has quietly become a way of avoiding Him.

### Twice Cleansed

And here is a detail worth noticing: Jesus did this twice.

John places a cleansing at the beginning of His ministry (John 2:13–16), and the synoptic gospels place one at the end (Matthew 21:12–13). Two cleansings, three years apart.

Read that carefully and it becomes a doctrine of hope. **The house needed cleansing more than once, and the One who cleansed it did not walk away the second time.** If your last attempt at reordering your life ended in the same clutter, that is not disqualification; it is the ordinary maintenance of a house that has people in it.

### The House You Are

And now the turn that makes this a New Testament teaching rather than an architectural one.

"What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?" (1 Corinthians 6:19).

The temple is not a building you visit. It is a body you live in. And Peter adds the corporate dimension: "Ye also, as lively stones, are built up a spiritual house, an holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 2:5).

So the name transfers. **Your life is now the house called by His name, and the same question applies: is prayer what happens here, and is the door open?**

### The House and the Open Door

And note the "for all people" clause again, because it is the part we are most likely to drop.

A house of prayer for all people means the stranger gets in. **The Ministry of Reconciliation** is what makes a person's praying available to someone who has no claim on it — and it means the church's prayer meeting is not a private club for the already-certain.

The test is concrete. Would a person in crisis, with no church background, no vocabulary and no idea what to say, find their way into the prayer of your house? Or would they find a corridor, and a queue, and a form to fill in?

### Keeping the House

One illustration, and then the practicalities.

A hearth is not a fireplace. A fireplace is a piece of masonry; a hearth is the place where the fire is kept and the family gathers. You can build a fireplace in a day. You keep a hearth by putting wood on it.

**And a hearth that is not fed becomes a shelf.** Not through any decision, and not with any announcement — just gradually, as other things find a use for the space.

That is what happens to prayer in a life. Nobody decides to stop; the space simply gets used for something else. And here faith and wisdom work together, because the space gets used by real duties that must still be done: the house still needs cleaning, the work still needs finishing, and the answer is ordering, not abandoning.

So the keeping is unremarkable and continuous: the hour that does not move, the sentence in the ordinary moment, the door left open for whoever comes. And the honest guard against turning the house into a museum of your own discipline — a **Presence of God** that is real is a place people get fed, not a place they admire the furniture.

## What This Means for You

**1. Check the name.** Is the thing your life is actually named for prayer? Not intended for — named for. What do people find when they come to you?

**2. Look for the corridor.** Where has the space set apart for God become a thoroughfare for other business? That is your cleansing, and it is probably not dramatic.

**3. Open the door to the one without a claim.** A house of prayer for all people will have somebody in it who does not fit. If it does not, the name has quietly changed.

**4. Expect to be cleansed twice.** The house needs maintenance, and the One who cleansed it the first time is not disgusted by the second.

**5. Let the Kingdom set the scope.** This is not your private chapel; it is a house in the **Kingdom of God**, and its door faces outward.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Isaiah 56:1–8** and mark who is being welcomed.
- **Read Matthew 21:12–17** and note what Jesus did after the cleansing.
- **Name the traffic** in your house, and move one thing out.
- **Pray with someone who does not know how.** That is the door.
