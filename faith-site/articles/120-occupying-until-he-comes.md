Luke 19 begins with a man in a tree, and ends with a king and ten servants and a reckoning. The chapter that gives us Zacchaeus also gives us the sharpest parable in the Gospels about what happens to authority over time.

"And as they heard these things, he added and spake a parable, because he was nigh to Jerusalem, and because they thought that the kingdom of God should immediately appear" (Luke 19:11).

That is the setting, and it matters. The crowd expected the kingdom to arrive at once, so Jesus told them a story about a delay. A nobleman goes to a far country to receive a kingdom, and he leaves his servants with something to do in the meantime.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **authority in the kingdom is never a possession to admire but a trust to trade — given by a departing King, accounted for at His return, and measured by what it produced rather than by how carefully it was preserved.**

## Ten Servants, Ten Minas

"He called his ten servants, and delivered them ten pounds, and said unto them, Occupy till I come" (Luke 19:13).

The word translated "pounds" is *mina*. A mina was about three months' wages — a real sum, not a token. Each servant received the same amount, which is a detail worth noticing: this parable is not about differing gifts, as the parable of the talents is. It is about equal trust and unequal response.

And the instruction, *pragmateusasthe* — "occupy" — is a business word. It means do business, trade, be engaged. It is the vocabulary of the marketplace, not the vocabulary of the shrine.

**The Authority of the Believer** — the right to act in His name — is what is being handed out here. **The Kingdom of God** — His rule, already present and still coming — is what is being extended through the trading. **Faith and Wisdom** — believing what God says and acting sensibly on it — is how the mina is put to work.

Consider a father who hands his teenage son the keys to the family shop for a fortnight and says, keep it trading. The son who polishes the keys and never opens the door has misunderstood the gift entirely.

### What "Occupy" Actually Requires

Occupy is a strange word for a modern ear, because it sounds passive — as though the servant were told to sit still and hold the fort.

Nothing could be further from the intent. The same root gives us the English word "practice." It means to be occupied with something, engaged in it, doing business with it.

So the command is not "guard the mina." It is "put the mina to work." Which means the servant's job in the King's absence is not to preserve a coin but to extend a business — the family business, which is the rule of God over the lives He has placed in reach.

### The Servant Who Buries It

The third servant is the one we must sit with, because he is the most religious figure in the story.

He wrapped the mina in a napkin and hid it. And when the reckoning comes, he explains himself: "For I feared thee, because thou art an austere man: thou takest up that thou layedst not down, and reapest that thou didst not sow" (Luke 19:21).

Read that carefully. His inaction did not come from laziness or unbelief about the King's return. **It came from a false picture of the King.** He thought him austere — harsh, grasping, unfair. And a man who believes that about God will always hide his talent, because trading it in such a world feels like exposure.

Set the two servants side by side and the contrast clarifies. One hears the same command and starts trading, expecting the master to be pleased by increase. The other hears it and buries, expecting the master to be predatory. Same mina, same instruction, two completely different gods in the room.

**Your view of the King governs your use of His authority.** That is the law operating under this whole parable.

### The Reckoning Is Specific

Notice the accounting is not vague. "Then came the first, saying, Lord, thy pound hath gained ten pounds" (Luke 19:16). And the second: "thy pound hath gained five pounds" (Luke 19:18).

Two things stand out. First, the reward is proportioned — ten cities for ten minas, five for five. The King is not embarrassed by different returns. Second, the servants measure their increase against the mina they were given, not against each other.

That releases you from a comparison you were never asked to make. The question at the reckoning is not what your brother did with his. It is what became of yours.

### The Steward's Honest Ledger

There is a warning inside the encouragement, and it belongs here because this is a teaching about authority.

Paul writes, "Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God. Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful" (1 Corinthians 4:1–2). Faithful, not impressive. And then, a few verses on, "He that judgeth me is the Lord" (1 Corinthians 4:4).

Which means the steward's internal ledger is not the final one. You may think you have done poorly and be commended. You may think you have done well and find the motive tested. The reckoning belongs to the King, and it is both kinder and more exact than our own self-assessment.

### And the Kingdom Was Received

One detail closes the loop on the whole month. The nobleman went away "to receive for himself a kingdom" — and in the story, he does. He returns having received it.

So the delay is not an absence of rule. It is the period in which the rule is being extended through stewards. You are not waiting for the kingdom to start; you are trading within it while the King is away receiving what is already His.

## What This Means for You

**1. Identify your mina.** What has been entrusted to you — time, money, a home, a voice, a skill, a position, a family? Name it plainly. A trust unnamed cannot be traded.

**2. Repent of the austere picture.** Which of God's commands do you quietly avoid because you suspect He is harsh? That suspicion is the napkin.

**3. Trade this week.** Do one concrete thing with what you have: a conversation, a gift, a step of obedience, an honest day's work done as unto Him.

**4. Expect increase, not applause.** The servants were promoted into cities. Stewardship produces responsibility rather than applause, and responsibility is the better gift.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the King is returning and is not austere toward those He has accepted. Wisdom says a steward keeps records, plans sensibly, avoids reckless ventures, provides for his household, and does not confuse boldness with presumption. Faith trades; wisdom counts the cost.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Luke 19:11–27 and Matthew 25:14–30 together**, and note what differs between the two parables.
- **Write down your mina** — the specific thing God has entrusted to you — and one way to put it to work this week.
- **Name the fear** that has kept it wrapped, and answer it with what Scripture says about the King.
- **Read 2 Corinthians 5:9–10**, and let the judgment seat settle your ambition.
