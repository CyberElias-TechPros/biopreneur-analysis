Matthew 7 closes the Sermon on the Mount, and just before the two builders Jesus gives three imperatives in a row.

"Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7).

Three verbs, and they are not synonyms. They escalate.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **petition is not a vague sigh in the direction of heaven but a specific request brought to a specific Father — and the specificity is itself an act of faith, because only someone who expects an answer bothers to name what he wants.**

## Three Verbs, Not One

**Ask** — *aiteite*, a present imperative: keep asking. It is the ordinary word for requesting something from someone who has it.

**Seek** — *zēteite*, to search for, to look for. This is what you do when the thing is not in your hand.

**Knock** — *krouete*, to strike a door. This is what you do when access is the issue.

The progression is deliberate. Asking is verbal; seeking is active; knocking is persistent. They describe a person who is not merely hoping but is engaged.

**Communion With the Holy Spirit** — the relationship that makes asking possible — is the ground. **The Life of Faith** — expecting an answer — is what specificity requires. **Confession of the Word** — saying what God says — is how the request is framed.

Consider a man who tells a builder "I would like the house to be better somehow," and a man who hands over a drawing. Only one of them has actually asked for a house.

### Why Specific Is Faith

There is a mechanism here that is easy to miss.

A vague request costs nothing and risks nothing. If nothing happens, you can say you were not asking for that in particular. It is a way of praying that protects you from disappointment — and it is also a way of praying that cannot be answered recognisably.

A specific request commits you. You have named the thing, and now the outcome will be visible either way.

Set the two prayers side by side. One believer prays "bless my family" for twenty years and cannot tell you what changed. Another prays for a specific son, a specific habit, a specific conversation — and can tell you what happened.

### The Promise Is Repeated Three Times

Verses 7 and 8 say the same thing twice, but the second time with a universal qualifier.

"For every one that asketh receiveth; and he that seeketh findeth; and to him that knocketh it shall be opened."

*Every one.* That is a wide promise, and it is given without a list of conditions attached in the immediate text. Which means the integrity of the promise is important to Jesus — He states it, then restates it, then illustrates it.

### The Illustration: A Father and a Fish

Matthew 7:9–11 supplies the argument, and it is an argument from ordinary goodness.

"What man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, will he give him a stone? Or if he ask a fish, will he give him a serpent?"

And then the point: "If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?" (Matthew 7:11).

The comparison is not between a good father and a better one. It is between flawed human fathers and God. If imperfect parents know how to feed a hungry child, the arithmetic of the "much more" is obvious.

### The Limits That Scripture States Elsewhere

An honest article has to say what the rest of the New Testament says, because this promise has been preached into disappointment.

James names the failure mode: "Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts" (James 4:3). Motive matters.

John names the boundary: "And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us" (1 John 5:14). The request is answered within His will, not instead of it.

And Paul gives the hardest case: he asked three times for a thorn to be removed, and the answer was no plus grace (2 Corinthians 12:8–9).

None of that weakens the promise. It locates it. A father who says yes to bread does not say yes to a serpent, and a son who trusts his father does not read the refusal of a serpent as neglect.

### Philippians Adds the Posture

Paul's version of petition adds something Jesus's phrasing does not.

"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:6–7).

*Be careful for nothing* — stop being anxious about everything. And the alternative is not passivity. It is making your requests known, with thanksgiving.

Note what is promised: not that every request is granted, but that the peace of God keeps the heart. Petition and peace are meant to travel together.

### How to Ask Precisely

Practically, precision is a discipline of three questions.

**What exactly am I asking for?** If the answer is a feeling rather than a fact, sharpen it.

**What would it look like if God answered this?** If you cannot describe it, you have not defined it.

**What am I asking according to?** A promise in the Word, or a preference?

### The Honest Boundary

If you have asked specifically and nothing has come, this article will not tell you that you lacked faith.

Jesus, in the same sermon, said the narrow gate is hard and that the rain falls on both houses. The promise is real, and the timetable is not yours. Keep asking — the verb is continuous — and let the asking itself hold you in the relationship.

## What This Means for You

**1. Write the request down.** Specificity on paper is specificity in prayer.

**2. Ask for things you can recognise.** If the answer could never be identified, redefine the request.

**3. Keep asking.** The verb is present tense. Persistence is part of the instruction.

**4. Add thanksgiving.** It changes the posture from demand to trust.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says ask, and keep asking. Wisdom says check the motive, submit to His will, and remember that a father's no to a serpent is as loving as his yes to bread.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 7:7–12** three times this week.
- **Write three specific requests** and pray them daily.
- **Read James 4:1–3**, and test your motives.
- **Thank God for one answer** before you ask for the next.
