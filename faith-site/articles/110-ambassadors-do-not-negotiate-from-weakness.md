An ambassador does not apply for the job of being heard. He arrives with credentials, and the receiving nation either accepts them or does not. The message he carries does not change in authority depending on how the foreign minister treats him that morning.

Paul uses exactly this image for the believer: "Now then we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us: we pray you in Christ's stead, be ye reconciled to God" (2 Corinthians 5:20).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **an ambassador's weight comes from the government that sent him, not from the welcome of the country he stands in — so the gospel is announced with authority and offered with pleading, never with apology.**

## Two Sounds in One Verse

Read the verse again and notice that it contains two very different sounds, and both are crucial.

"We are ambassadors for Christ" — that is the sound of authority. It is a statement of standing and commission.
"We pray you in Christ's stead, be ye reconciled to God" — that is the sound of pleading. It is a request, made with tears in it.

Most of us can manage one or the other. Which sound do you default to when you are nervous? Some believers carry the authority sound and become cold, correct, and impossible to approach. Others carry the pleading sound and become apologetic, unsure, and easy to ignore. Paul carries both in a single sentence, because the message itself demands both.

**The Ministry of Reconciliation** — God's work of bringing humanity back into fellowship with Himself, entrusted to believers — is what an ambassador is delivering. **The Authority of the Believer** — the right to stand in His name — is the credentials that make the delivery official. **The Name of Jesus** — His person, work, and authority — is the Name in which the appeal is made.

Consider a passport. It contains almost nothing but paper, ink, and a photograph, and its holder's personal strength is irrelevant to whether a border opens for him. The document carries the weight of the state that issued it because he carries the document.

### The Word Behind the Word

The Greek word is *presbeuo* — to be an elder, and by extension to act as an official representative. It was used of the envoys that cities sent to negotiate with other states, and there is a tradition of using it for messengers sent to discuss peace.

That last part should land hard. Paul's image is not of a herald announcing a victory, though that is also true elsewhere in the New Testament. It is of a representative sent to a city in conflict to discuss terms. And the terms he carries are stunningly favourable to the city.

Look at his other use of the word, in a letter written from prison: "for which I am an ambassador in bonds: that therein I may speak boldly, as I ought to speak" (Ephesians 6:20). The chains are not a contradiction of the ambassadorship; they are part of its cost. He asks for boldness while chained, which tells you that weakness of circumstance and weakness of message are two entirely different things.

### Why the Ambassador Cannot Apologise for the Message

Here is where the church has lost so much ground, usually with good intentions.

An ambassador is not authorised to improve the offer. He cannot soften terms because the receiving country finds them distasteful. He cannot announce that the King's conditions have changed. He is a courier of a decision already made. And who authorised you to renegotiate the terms of a peace you did not purchase?

That rules out several things that have become common.
It rules out presenting the gospel as one lifestyle option among others that the hearer may reasonably consider, because an ambassador does not negotiate with a subject over whether the kingdom exists.
It rules out removing the demand for repentance in order to avoid a bad reaction, because the message already assumed His name.
It rules out treating rejection as though it invalidated the message — which is the particular mistake of believers whose sense of validation comes from the reception rather than the sending.

Now notice what it does not rule out. It does not licence harshness. Paul's own tone here is pleading, not commanding. The ambassador's authority is exercised in the *content* of the message, not in his manner toward the hearer.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. One believer, believing his message to be only a suggestion, edits it into something universally agreeable and wonders why nobody changes. Another, believing his stance to be his own, defends the message as though he were its author and leaves a trail of wounded people. The ambassador is neither. He says exactly what he was sent to say, with the demeanour of a man who is glad he was allowed to carry it.

### The Message in Acts 17

Paul's sermon in Athens is the model for speaking to people who do not share your categories.

He had been in the city and seen its idols, and Luke records what kind of feeling that produced: "his spirit was stirred in him, when he saw the city wholly given to idolatry" (Acts 17:16). Stirred — vexed, provoked. Then he went to the synagogue, to the market, and to the philosophers, and spoke.

He did not begin with a warning about hell. He began with something true about them: he had looked at their altars and found one inscribed "To the unknown god," and he declared that the God they were worshipping in ignorance was the one he now announced (Acts 17:23).

Then comes the sentence that shows me the difference. "For in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28) — Paul quotes their own poets, and the God of the whole earth is shown closer to them than they thought.

But he does not stop there, and this is where so much modern teaching goes wrong. He goes on to call for repentance, and he names the judgement and the resurrection (Acts 17:30–31). Some mocked. Some said they would hear again. A few believed.

The ambassador adapted the approach and never adapted the message. That distinction is the whole skill.

## What This Means for You

**1. Sort out where your weight comes from before you speak.** If your confidence depends on how the conversation goes, you will either soften the message to preserve the warmth or turn cold to protect yourself. Your standing is settled in Christ, not in the room.

**2. Remove the apology but keep the gentleness.** There is no need to say "I know this sounds old-fashioned" or to laugh at your own conviction before anyone else can. Say the thing plainly and kindly, and let people have a real thing to respond to.

**3. Learn the difference between the message and the method.** The crucifixion and resurrection are not negotiable. Whether you say it over coffee, in a hospital corridor, or with a passage from their own poets is entirely flexible. Adapt everything but the announcement.

**4. Expect some to walk away, and do not read it as your failure.** Paul had mockers in Athens. He also had conversions. What he asked for was boldness, not a result.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom honour the hearer's dignity.** Faith says the message is true for everyone. Wisdom considers whether your words are timely, whether the listener is a stranger or a friend, whether the tone is a servant's or a conqueror's. Faith and wisdom work together: faith refuses to change the gospel, wisdom refuses to change the person's dignity to make a point.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 2 Corinthians 5:14–21 slowly** and underline every word that talks about exchange or reconciliation.
- **Write your own account of the message in three sentences** — what is wrong, what was done, what is asked — then check it against the actual gospel rather than against a version you inherited.
- **Have one ordinary conversation this week** in which you are simply honest about your faith, without pressure, without apology.
- **Read Acts 17:16–34** and note what Paul adapted and what he never adapted.
