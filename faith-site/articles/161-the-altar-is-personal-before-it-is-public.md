Matthew 6 contains a sentence that quietly rearranges the whole idea of a religious life.

"But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret" (Matthew 6:6).

A closet. A shut door. No audience.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the altar is built in private and only then attended in public — the shut door is not a restriction on your worship but the workshop where it is made.**

## The Word "Closet"

The Greek is *tameion* — an inner room, a storeroom, the place where a household kept what it valued.

It is a small word for a small space. And Jesus chooses it on purpose, because the whole point of the contrast in Matthew 6 is that there is a place with no crowd in it.

**Communion With the Holy Spirit** — the fellowship of that room — is what is built there. **The Life of Faith** — believing without an audience — is what it requires. **The Presence of God** — the reality that fills the closed room — is the reward the verse names.

Consider a musician who practises in a room nobody enters. The concert is not the practice, and the practice is not the concert. But remove the room and the concert becomes noise.

### The Three Contrasts

Matthew 6 makes the same argument about three practices, and the structure is identical each time.

**Giving:** "Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, to be seen of them" (Matthew 6:1).

**Praying:** "thou shalt not be as the hypocrites are: for they love to pray standing in the synagogues and in the corners of the streets" (Matthew 6:5).

**Fasting:** "be not, as the hypocrites, of a sad countenance" (Matthew 6:16).

Three practices, one warning: an audience ruins them. Not because public things are wrong, but because a practice performed *for* an audience has already received its payment — "They have their reward" (Matthew 6:2, 5, 16).

### The Word "Hypocrite"

It is worth knowing what Jesus actually said. The Greek is *hypokritēs* — an actor, a person playing a role behind a mask.

The problem is not that the man praying on the street corner is pretending to pray. He is praying. The problem is that the prayer is a performance for a different audience than the one addressed.

Set the two prayers side by side. One man prays in the street so that people will think he is devout, and the prayer is real but the audience is wrong. Another prays in a closed room with no witness at all, and the only audience is God. Both are praying. Only one is a conversation.

### Why the Secret Place Is Harder

An honest word here about the effort, because this sounds easier than it is.

Public prayer has supports. There is a structure, a role, an expectation, and other people's voices carrying you. The shut door removes all of it.

Which means the secret place is where your actual appetite for God is revealed. And that is precisely why it is valuable — not because God needs to see it, but because you do. What does your shut door tell you?

### Daniel's Window

Daniel is the great example, and the detail is worth noticing.

He knew the decree against praying, "and he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (Daniel 6:10).

*As he did aforetime.* The window was open and the habit was already established. The public crisis was met by a private routine that predated it.

That is the order this teaching describes. The altar was personal first; the courage was a byproduct.

### "Shall Reward Thee Openly"

The promise is real, and it needs careful handling.

Jesus says the Father who sees in secret will reward openly. That is not a transaction — pray in private and get a public blessing. It is a description of how God works: the hidden thing grows, and eventually it shows.

But notice that the reward is God's to define, and it may not look like what an audience would notice. Very often the reward is that you changed.

### How an Altar Is Built

An altar in Scripture is a particular place — a stone, a heap, a spot someone returned to.

For most believers, that means a time and a place, not necessarily a room. A chair, a corner, a walk. What matters is that it is settled and yours.

The pattern is unremarkable: the same time, the same place, the same honesty before the same God. The altar is not impressive. It is frequent. Is yours frequent enough to be one?

### The Honest Boundary

A word for people whose room is never quiet.

If you have small children, a shift pattern, a shared flat, or a season of illness, a "closet" may not be available as a physical place. The point of the verse is the absence of an audience, not the presence of four walls.

A quiet walk, a car before the gate opens, ten minutes in a stairwell — the shut door can be a shut door and still be a stairwell.

## What This Means for You

**1. Find your *tameion*.** A place, a time, and a settled expectation that is yours.

**2. Shut the door deliberately.** Phone off, notifications gone. The door is part of the practice.

**3. Do it when it is not exciting.** The altar is built in the unremarkable weeks.

**4. Let public things come out of it.** Whatever you offer in the gathering, offer out of what was built in the room.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the secret room is where God works. Wisdom says that means a sustainable habit rather than a heroic one — a small time that actually happens beats a grand plan that never does.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 6:1–8** slowly, and note the three repetitions of "they have their reward."
- **Choose a place and time**, and go there every day this week.
- **Shut your phone away** and leave it off for the duration.
- **Read Daniel 6:10**, and note the phrase "as he did aforetime."
