Jesus once stood in front of a fig tree with nothing but leaves on it and said, "No man eat fruit of thee hereafter for ever" (Mark 11:14). There was no crowd, no audience, no opportunity to impress anyone. Peter noticed the result the next morning, and Jesus used the moment to teach the disciples about speech.

Read the sequence, because it is not a lesson about horticulture. He spoke. It happened. He explained.

And the explanation is one of the most extraordinary sentences in the Gospels: "whosoever shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed... and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have whatsoever he saith" (Mark 11:23). Here is the central truth of this teaching: **authority in the kingdom is exercised through speech — not because words are magical, but because a word is how a person made in God's image takes a public stand on God's side.**

## Why Speech and Not Feeling

Notice how many times the verse uses the word "say." Say to the mountain. Believe what he says. Have whatsoever he says. Mark's account is almost clumsy with repetition, and the clumsiness is the point, because the whole mechanism of the verse is verbal.

Why would God build authority into speech rather than into sincerity of intention?

Because speech is the point at which a private conviction becomes a public position. You can hold an opinion indefinitely and it costs you nothing. The moment you say it aloud, you have committed yourself to a side. That is why Romans 10 joins the two so tightly: "if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart... thou shalt be saved. For with the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation" (Romans 10:9–10).

**The Authority of the Believer** — the right to stand in His name — becomes operative at the point of **Confession of the Word**, *homologia*, saying the same thing God says. And what makes the saying weighty is not the sound of the voice but the fact that it is grounded in **the Word of God**, which is the thing being agreed with.

Think of a barrister rising in court to enter a plea. The words themselves are ordinary English. Their force comes from the fact that he is an officer of the court, standing in the right place, saying the thing that is actually true of the case.

### The Fig Tree and the Timing

There is an oddity in Mark's account worth pausing over, because it protects the doctrine from becoming a technique. The fig tree had leaves, and Mark notes that "the time of figs was not yet" (Mark 11:13). Jesus was not confused about the season. He was using a living thing to teach a lesson about a nation and about faith, in the way the prophets had done for centuries.

More importantly, look at what is happening in the same chapter. Jesus goes on to cleanse the temple, and the next day the fig tree is withered from the roots. Mark is deliberately braiding two stories, and both concern something that looked alive from a distance. That is not incidental. It is a warning about a religious appearance that has green leaves and no fruit.

So the lesson on speaking is set inside a lesson on authenticity. Faith that speaks is not a substitute for being real before God; it is the expression of a person who is.

### What Kind of Words

Mark's verse has a condition, and it is not a formula. "Shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass." The heart is in the middle of it. Jesus is not teaching that the right sequence of syllables rearranges reality; He is teaching that a mouth which says something the heart has already settled is in a different category from a mouth that is hoping.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. There are words that describe a situation back to God in the language of despair, and they are honestly spoken. Then there are words that say what God has said, and the heart quite simply agrees. One is a report. The other is a verdict. Both are speech, and only one of them is authority.

James spends a whole chapter on the same organ and makes the same point from the other end: "the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things" (James 3:5). He observes that a ship is turned by a small helm, and then adds the uncomfortable part — that the same mouth blesses God and curses men, and that this ought not to be. The problem is not that words are too weak to matter. It is that they are too strong to be spoke carelessly.

### The Speaking That Is Not a Show

There is a real danger in all of this, and it usually presents itself as zeal. A believer learns that faith speaks, and within a week the whole room has heard about mountains being cast into the sea. Then the mountain is still there, and now there is confusion as well.

Ezekiel 37 gives the corrective. The prophet is set in a valley of dry bones and told to prophesy: "Prophesy upon these bones, and say unto them, O ye dry bones, hear the word of the LORD" (Ezekiel 37:4). He does not promise them flesh in his own name. He speaks the word of the Lord to them, and the doing is the Lord's.

There is a further detail: the bones come together, and there is still no breath, and God must act again. The prophet's speech was necessary, and it was not sufficient. He did not treat his own mouth as the source of life.

That is the boundary this teaching needs. You speak because God has spoken, and you speak the thing He said. You do not speak the thing you would like, and then accuse Him of failing when the sky stays stubbornly unchanged.

### The Practical Shape

So what does speaking authority actually look like in an ordinary week? Less dramatic than the sermons suggest, and more deliberate than most of us are.

First, it is specific. Jesus said, "Be thou removed" to a mountain, and "No man eat fruit of thee" to a tree. Vagueness is where most of us go wrong, and it is usually a symptom of unresolved thinking rather than a lack of faith.

Second, it is consistent. Speak to your temptation out loud on the day it is strong, and watch whether you say the same thing with your words at midnight that you said at breakfast.

Third, it is sparing. James says the tongue is a fire, and a fire is dangerous precisely when it is unsupervised. A person learning to speak with authority does not talk more. They often talk less, because they have noticed that their words now matter.

## What This Means for You

**1. Say the thing you actually believe, out loud.** For most believers the gap is not between faith and doubt but between faith and the unspoken. Say what the Word says, in words, today — not because the air needs rearranging, but because you need to be committed.

**2. Refuse to narrate the problem on its own terms.** How long are you going to say the thing you are trying to be free of? The constant recital of a difficulty is not honesty; it is the spoken form of a verdict you would deny in daylight. Name the fact, then state the truth that outranks it.

**3. Do not use authority as a way of avoiding obedience.** Somebody has to go to the doctor, and somebody has to do the reconciliation, and somebody has to finish the work. Faith and wisdom work together here: faith speaks to the situation, wisdom takes the next practical step. Faith speaks and wisdom then does; a person who speaks and does nothing has confused confession with competence.

**4. Watch what you say about people.** Psalm 139:4 says, "there is not a word in my tongue, but, lo, O Lord, thou knowest it altogether." If the Lord knows every word, they are real acts, and the authority attached to your words is not only available for spiritual warfare. It is also available for blessing, and Scripture is far more interested in that than we are.

**5. If you have anything to speak, take the Lord's own words for it.** Jesus answered the wilderness with "It is written." He did not answer in His own voice. A word you have taken from Scripture is a stand you have already been given the legal right to take.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Mark 11:12–26 in one sitting** and note how the fig tree and the temple are related.
- **Write the one thing you say about your difficulty** and then write what the Word says about it, and speak the second out loud twice a day.
- **Pick one careless sentence** you catch yourself saying this week and replace it deliberately.
- **Read Ezekiel 37:1–10** and count how many times God's action is needed after the prophet speaks.
