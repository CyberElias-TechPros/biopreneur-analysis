Every nativity scene has the same silent detail in it, and it is not the animals or the star: somebody turned a family away on the night the Son of God was born.

"And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn."

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **Luke's word is *katalyma* — a guest room — and the tragedy of Bethlehem was not a lack of buildings but a lack of hospitality, which is why the question the verse keeps asking is whether there is room in you.**

## What Actually Happened at the Inn

### The Word Is Guest Room

And *katalyma* is not a word for a commercial hotel. It is the word Luke uses for the upper room where the Passover was prepared (Luke 22:11), and the word the Septuagint uses for a lodging place.

It means a room in a house. A guest room.

Which means the "innkeeper" of the Christmas play may never have existed. There may simply have been a full house.

### A Village Full of Relatives

And Bethlehem was Joseph's ancestral town. He was of the house and lineage of David, going up to the city of David to be taxed (Luke 2:4).

There were relatives in that village. The guest room was taken, and the family with the pregnant wife got the animals' quarters.

**It was not hostility that shut Him out. It was crowding.** And which is more likely to shut Him out of your house tonight?

### What a Manger Implies

And the detail Luke includes without comment is the manger — a feeding trough. If the guest room was full, the lower part of the house, where the animals were brought in at night, was the only warm place left.

The King of the universe was born in the garage because the house was busy.

## The Longer Pattern

### He Came to His Own

And John says it as a summary of a whole nation's response: "He came unto his own, and his own received him not" (John 1:11).

Bethlehem was not an isolated incident. It was a rehearsal.

### The Standing Invitation

And the risen Christ still speaks in the language of a doorstep: "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him" (Revelation 3:20).

Notice He is still outside. Still knocking. Still not forcing the latch.

**He has never once kicked a door down.**

### What Fills the House

And the reason there was no room was not wickedness on that particular night. It was that everything in the house already had an occupant.

That is a diagnostic for a heart, not a verdict on a village.

### What Takes the Room

### Crowding, Not Rebellion

And most of us are not hostile to Christ. We are simply full. Full of work, of worry, of a hundred legitimate claims on the calendar.

The most common rival to Jesus is not a scandal. It is a schedule.

### Comfort and Control

And two guests are especially hard to evict. Comfort, which resists any arrangement that costs something. Control, which wants to know how the house will be run.

Both of them say they are only staying a short while.

### Even Good Things

And here is the subtlety: the room can be filled with ministry, with study, with serving. Martha was busy preparing a meal for the Lord while Mary sat at His feet (Luke 10:40).

Busyness for Him is not the same as room for Him.

**It is possible to be exhausted in His service and empty of His presence.**

### Making Room

### Clear the Space

And the first act of hospitality is subtraction. Something has to go, and it is usually a lesser thing that has taken a larger place than it deserved.

Name it, and put it down for a season.

### Invite Him In Specifically

And the second act is invitation. Not a vague openness, but a named room — the finances, the marriage, the body, the fear.

He does not want the entryway. He wants the house.

### Give Him the Owner's Room

And the third is the hard one. Guests get a room; owners get the house. Jesus did not come to be accommodated; He came to be Lord.

Psalm 24 says the King of glory shall come in — and the gates have to be lifted up.

### Keep Room for Him Daily

And finally, hospitality is a habit. A room offered once and then re-let is not a room offered.

**Yesterday's hospitality is not today's room.**

### The Boundary

Two guardrails.

First, this is not a demand for emotional perfection before He will come in. He came to a manger. He is not waiting for the house to be renovated; He is asking for the key.

Second, making room is not passivity. Faith and wisdom clear the calendar, keep the commitments that matter, and still leave the first hours to Him.

## What This Means for You

1. Read Luke 2:1–20 and count how many people are mentioned as responding to the news.

2. Ask: what has taken the room Jesus should have?

3. Name one occupancy and evict it this week — a commitment, a habit, or a fear.

4. Give Him one named area, out loud, in specific words.

5. **Clear the space, and keep faith and wisdom together.** Hospitality is not chaos. Keep your duties, honour your obligations, and still let the first place belong to Him.

### Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Revelation 3:14–22.** Note who is standing outside.
- **Read Luke 10:38–42.** Note the difference between the two sisters.
- **Leave one evening unscheduled for Him this week.**
- **Open the door in prayer, in words, every morning.**
- **Notice what you reach for when you are anxious — that is often the occupied room.**

**The central truth, said plainly:** Luke's word is *katalyma* — a guest room — and the tragedy of Bethlehem was not a lack of buildings but a lack of hospitality, which is why the question the verse keeps asking is whether there is room in you.

**Declare this today:**

> "Father, I do not want to be a closed door to Your Son. Search the rooms of my heart, name what has crowded Him out, and help me make room for Jesus today — not as a guest passing through, but as Lord, in His Name. Amen."
