This is the hardest passage in the New Testament for anyone who believes God heals, and it is in Paul's own hand.

"For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:8–9).

A man who had seen the dead raised asked three times to be relieved of something and was told no. He then wrote the account himself for our benefit.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **grace is not the consolation prize awarded when the answer is withheld; it is a working strength that arrives inside the weakness and does what the removal would have done.**

## What the Thorn Was

### Naming the Thing Honestly

Paul does not tell us what it was, and the guesses are old: eye trouble, malaria, epilepsy, a speech impediment, a persistent opponent.

What he does tell us is more useful than the diagnosis.

He calls it "a thorn in the flesh" — *skolops*, a sharp stake or splinter, something that pierces. He calls it "the messenger of Satan" — *angelos satana*, an angel of the adversary. And he says it was given "lest I should be exalted above measure" (2 Corinthians 12:7).

Three things in one sentence, and they sit awkwardly. It pierces. It is satanic in origin. And it served God's purpose.

That tension is where most teaching on this passage collapses into one of two errors. **One camp says everything painful is God's will, and the other says nothing painful can ever be permitted. Paul refuses both by holding all three facts at once.**

### Why He Was Refused

The reason given is astonishing in its smallness: "lest I should be exalted above measure."

Paul had been shown things not lawful to be uttered — caught up to the third heaven, hearing unspeakable words (2 Corinthians 12:2–4). A man given that could become insufferable. The thorn was a leash.

So the refusal was not a failure of faith. It was a mercy aimed at the man's character, and the man was not necessarily the best judge of what he needed.

That is worth saying gently, because it lands on the reader who has prayed for years for one thing. Not every unanswered request is moral failure. Sometimes the refusal is protection from something you cannot see.

### What "Sufficient" Actually Means

The answer given is where the passage turns, and the word *arkei* is doing heavy work.

"Sufficient" — *arkei*, from *arkeō*: to be enough, to suffice, to be a match for. It is not a sympathetic word. It is a word of adequacy.

**God did not say "I am sorry." He said "I am enough."** There is a difference between consolation and sufficiency, and the second one is much stronger than the first.

And notice the structure of the reply. Paul asked for a change in his circumstances, and God answered with a Person. Not a no to the request so much as a redirect to the resource.

### "Strength Is Made Perfect" — the Tense of It

"Strength" here is *dynamis* — power, ability, the word behind "dynamite." "Made perfect" is *teleō*: to complete, to bring to its end, to finish.

The tense is present: it is being perfected, continuously. And that means the thorn is not the obstacle to the power; **the thorn is the location of it.**

Paul is precise about this: "that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Corinthians 12:9). "Rest upon" is *episkēnoō* — to pitch a tent over. The same root family as the tabernacle.

The power is not merely given; it camps on you. Where the weakness is, the tent goes up.

### The Thorn and the Healer

Which brings the necessary honesty, because this passage can be used to settle a matter it was never meant to settle.

The understanding of the Word we teach is that healing is providential provision for the church and that we are to pray for it with faith and expect it — James 5 tells us plainly, "The prayer of faith shall save the sick" (James 5:15). We do not hedge on that, and this article is not a retreat from it.

But 2 Corinthians 12 is a real account of a real refusal, and it does not permit us to demand that every infirmity be removed on our schedule. Two things are true together: healing is available in Christ, and Paul did not receive it in one instance.

A faith that can only hold one of those is a small faith. One produces a woman who says God did not heal her much-loved grandmother and therefore is not a healer. The other produces a man who tells a sick believer that his unanswered prayer must be unbelief. Both are wounded by a God they have boxed. Which box have you been building for Him?

### How the Thorn Stays a Thorn

There is a practical question underneath all of this that has to be answered, because otherwise we merely say beautiful things.

Paul's own answer was not denial. He kept the infirmity and stopped it being his identity.

"Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Read that word "glory" — *kauchaomai*, to boast. He is not pretending the thorn feels pleasant. He is choosing what it means.

Then he makes the declaration: "Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong" (2 Corinthians 12:10).

That is a man who has taken the thorn into the accounting and made it serve. The thorn did not become pleasant. It became productive.

### The Anchor That Holds

One illustration, and then the applications.

Think of a tent under a strong wind. A pole short of the pole it should be does not hold the tent. The peg does — and the peg is driven *into* the weakness, into the wet ground, at the place of the vacuum.

That is close to *episkēnoō*. The tent is not held up by the pole's beauty; it is held by its pegs. **The strength camps at the weakest point, and the weakest point is exactly why the tent stays up in weather the better-looking ones collapse in.**

Paul's thorn kept him at the peg, and the church is still sheltering under that tent.

### Some Words for Someone Specific

If you have been carrying one request for years, three things need saying.

First, keep asking. Paul asked three times; the number is not a warning. Asking again is not a lack of faith, and stopping is not a sign of maturity.

Would you recognise a sufficiency if it came without a removal? Second, do not let a refusal become a verdict about you. Nothing in 2 Corinthians 12 says Paul was a lesser apostle. The thorn was sent to a man with too much revelation, not too little.

Third, let the grace search for you. **The Life of Faith is not believing that the thorn is gone; it is believing that you will find enough grace in the exact place it sits.** That is a different belief, and it is the one Paul had.

And practical wisdom still stands: an infirmity that could be treated should be treated. Grace is sufficient, and grace often comes wearing a doctor's coat, a friend's car, or a medication's ordinary help. **Faith and wisdom work together** — and no one honours God by refusing the help He has already provided while asking for the strength to manage without it.

## What This Means for You

**1. Separate sufficiency from removal** God's most common answer is not a change in your circumstances but a resource in them. Ask which answer you would recognise.

**2. Hunt for the grace.** The **Power of the Holy Spirit** is not a mood to feel but a strength to find. Look for what is able in you where you thought you had nothing.

**3. Take the thorn into account.** Whatever you are carrying, name one thing it has made possible. That is your boast.

**4. Hold wholeness, not dates.** Wholeness belongs to you in Christ, and holding that hope is not arrogance. Both the promise and the waiting live in the same heart.

**5. Look for the tent.** If the power camps where you are weakest, then your weakest place is now a place to look for God rather than a place to hide from Him.
