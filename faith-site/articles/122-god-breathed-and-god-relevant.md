Paul writes his last letter to a young man he had trained, and near the end of it he makes a claim so large that it is easy to read past.

"All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).

"All." Not the favourite parts. Not the passages that preach easily. All of it. And then he attaches a purpose to that claim: "That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works" (2 Timothy 3:17).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **inspiration is not a claim about how old the Book is but about how far it reaches — every Scripture is God-breathed, and therefore every Scripture is equipped to do something in a life that nothing else can do.**

## One Word, and It Is a Compound

The Greek word translated "inspiration of God" is *theopneustos*. It is built from *theos* (God) and *pneō* (to breathe), and it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Paul appears to have coined it.

Note what the word does and does not say. It does not say the Scripture is *inspired* in the loose English sense of "inspiring" — moving, uplifting, the way a good speech is. It says the Scripture is *God-breathed*. The breath is not the effect the text has on you. The breath is where the text came from.

**The Word of God** — His self-expression in speech and writing — carries His own breath. **Revelation Knowledge** — truth made known rather than merely collected — is what that breath produces in a reader. **Renewing of the Mind** — the reshaping of a person's thinking — is what happens when the breath is not resisted.

Consider a glassblower. The vessel on the shelf is finished and hard, but it holds the shape it was given by breath. The shape is not a memory of breath. It is the residue of it.

### Why "Profitable" Is a Stronger Word Than It Sounds

The KJV's "profitable" is *ōphelimos* — useful, beneficial, advantageous. It is not a word about pleasantness. It is a word about function.

So Paul's argument runs like this. Because it is God-breathed, it is useful. The one claim produces the other. Inspiration is not a doctrine stored in a drawer; it is the reason the Book works.

That matters because a great many believers hold the doctrine and never use the Book. They will defend inspiration in an argument and leave the text unopened for a week. The doctrine was never given to be defended. It was given to be spent.

### Four Things It Does

Paul lists the profit in four parts, and the order is worth noticing.

**Doctrine** — what is true. **Reproof** — where you are wrong. **Correction** — how to get right. **Instruction in righteousness** — how to stay right.

That is a complete circuit, and it is diagnostic. A believer who only wants doctrine will grow accurate and stiff. One who only wants correction will grow discouraged. One who only wants instruction will grow busy without roots. The Book does all four, and it is meant to be allowed to do all four.

Set them side by side and the difference becomes plain. One reader comes to the text asking "what should I believe?" and leaves informed but unchanged. Another comes asking "what should I believe, where am I wrong, what do I do about it, and how do I keep walking?" and leaves furnished. Same Book, same hour, two different yields.

### The Verse That Follows

Verse 17 gives the goal, and it uses a word worth knowing: "throughly furnished" is *exartizō* — fully equipped, fitted out, complete in every part.

It is the word you would use of a ship fitted for a voyage or a workman with every tool for the job. And notice what it is attached to: "unto all good works."

So the purpose of Scripture is not to make you a person who has read a lot. It is to make you a person who is equipped. The Book is a workshop, not a museum.

### What About the Parts That Bother You?

Here is where honesty is needed, because "all Scripture" includes the genealogies, the laws about mildew, and the passages that make a modern reader wince.

Paul does not ask you to find every part equally warm. He asks you to treat every part as breathed and therefore useful. Sometimes the usefulness is immediate and obvious. Sometimes it is structural — the part tells you something about God's patience with a slow people, or about the seriousness of holiness, that no summary could carry.

There is a difference between a text you do not yet understand and a text you have decided in advance to skip. The first is a door. The second is a wall built by hand.

### A Book That Was Written Into a Life

There is one more detail in the passage, and it explains a great deal about why some people seem to get more out of Scripture than others.

Paul reminds Timothy of "the holy scriptures, which thou hast known from a child" (2 Timothy 3:15), and of the people who taught him. This was not a Book he discovered late and admired from a distance. It was woven into him over years, by a grandmother, a mother, and a mentor.

That does not mean late starters are excluded — it means the Book rewards the long habit. The people who find it furnishing them are usually the people who kept showing up while it was still mostly strange.

### The Sufficiency Question

If all Scripture is breathed and all of it profits, then a believer never has to ask whether God has said enough for the situation in front of him.

That is the practical edge of this doctrine. Not "is the Bible old and reliable?" but "is it enough for today?" And the answer Paul gives is that a man thoroughly furnished for every good work has been furnished by this.

## What This Means for You

**1. Read the parts you skip.** Pick one passage you have always avoided, and ask what it shows you about God rather than why it does not fit your expectations.

**2. Let all four functions work.** When you read, ask the four questions: what is true, where am I wrong, how do I turn, how do I keep walking?

**3. Treat the Book as equipment, not decoration.** What are you equipped to do this week that you were not equipped to do last month?

**4. Build the habit while it is still mostly strange.** Understanding is a harvest, not a precondition. Keep showing up.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says this is God's breath and it is sufficient. Wisdom says sufficiency does not mean you need no teachers, no counsel, no context, or no brothers and sisters — it means no other authority stands above it, and every other help is judged by it.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 2 Timothy 3:14–17 daily this week**, and take one of the four functions each day.
- **Memorise verse 16 in the translation you actually read.**
- **Name one passage you have avoided**, and read it with a notebook.
- **Read Psalm 19:7–11** and count how many effects the psalmist claims for the Word.
