There is a bottle of oil in a cupboard in a great many Christian homes, and its owner is not entirely sure what it is for. It is used in crisis, occasionally, with a great deal of embarrassment and a nagging suspicion that it might be superstition.

**James 5:14** is the verse behind the bottle: "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord."

Read the grammar, because the grammar settles most of the confusion. James does not say the oil heals. He says the elders are to pray and anoint, and then he tells us what does the work.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the oil was never the healer; it is a visible act of obedience that marks a person as set apart for God's attention, and the prayer of faith is what Scripture says saves the sick.**

## What the Oil Is Not

### Not a Medicine

Oil in the ancient world was used for wounds and for skin, and some read James as recommending a practical treatment. That reading is not absurd, but it cannot carry the verse, because the same passage goes on to say something a poultice cannot do: "And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up" (James 5:15).

So the oil accompanies something larger than itself. **It is a sign, not a substance.**

### Not a Charm

Here is the danger that has done real harm: treating the oil as a talisman. Rub it on and the sickness must leave. Say the words and the outcome is guaranteed.

That is not faith; it is a formula, and formulas are the opposite of faith because they remove the need to trust anyone. A charm works by being performed correctly. Faith works by leaning on a Person.

**The Anointing** in Scripture is never a substance you possess. It is God's presence and power at work, and the oil is a pointer to Him.

### Not a Replacement for the Body's Care

Some believers have used James 5 to refuse medical treatment, and that reading does violence to the passage. Notice that James assumes the sick person is doing something: *let him call for the elders*. He is already acting.

Faith and wisdom work together. Anointing and treatment belong to the same Father, and Scripture nowhere sets them against each other.

## What the Oil Is

### A Mark of Setting Apart

To understand the oil you have to go back to the tabernacle, where oil was used to consecrate — to set a person or an object apart for God's use. "Then shalt thou take the anointing oil, and pour it upon his head, and anoint him" (Exodus 29:7).

That is the background. When the elders anoint a sick believer, they are doing something visible with an invisible meaning: **this person is set apart for God's attention, not abandoned to their illness.**

### A Physical Act for a Whole Person

God has always dealt with people as whole beings. He could have said "pray and I will hear." Instead He gave a touchable act.

Think of how often He does this: water at baptism, bread and cup at communion, oil for the sick. Every one of them takes something ordinary and makes it a door for faith to walk through. **The gospel is not embarrassed by the physical** — the Word became flesh.

### Used in the Name

James adds the phrase that gives the act its entire weight: "in the name of the Lord."

The oil has no authority. The elder has no authority in himself. **The Name of Jesus** is where the authority lives, and the oil is applied *in* that Name — as an act done under it, not as a power beside it.

That is why the smallest, quietest anointing in a hospital room in the middle of the night is a serious act. Heaven is not watching the bottle. It is honouring the Name.

### What the Passage Actually Promises

Read verse 15 slowly: "And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him."

*Save* is *sōzō* — the same word used for salvation. It means to rescue, to make whole, to heal. James is not promising a mild improvement.

And notice the second clause. The passage links the body to the whole person: if sin is present, it is dealt with too. **Wholeness is the goal, not merely the absence of a symptom.**

### Why Elijah Is in the Same Paragraph

James does something surprising next. He moves from anointing oil to Elijah, a man with "like passions as we are," who prayed and the rain stopped for three and a half years (James 5:17).

Why put a prophet's prayer beside a sickroom? To make one point: **the prayer of faith is not a technique reserved for the impressive.** Elijah was a man like you, and he prayed earnestly.

## What This Means for You

**1. Call for the elders, and mean it.** The verse is an instruction, not a suggestion for the desperate. If you are sick, invite the church's leadership to pray with you.

**2. Do not let awkwardness rob you.** Most believers skip this because they feel foolish asking. James did not consider it foolish.

**3. Expect the prayer of faith, not the oil, to do the work.** Your confidence belongs in God's response to faith, not in the amount applied.

**4. Confess and be prayed for.** James says, "Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed" (James 5:16). Healing and honesty are connected in this passage.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** Anoint and treat. Pray and rest. Call the elders and keep the appointment. Faith and wisdom work together, and neither is diminished by the other.

### Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read James 5:13–18** and mark every verb.
- **If you are carrying illness**, ask two believing leaders to pray with you this month.
- **Read Exodus 30:22–33** and note what anointing meant.
- **Read Mark 6:7–13** and see the disciples' practice.
- **Pray verse 16** over someone else in your fellowship.
