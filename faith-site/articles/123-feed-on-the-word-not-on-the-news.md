Jeremiah had a day that would have ended most ministries.

He had preached, and the preaching had cost him. He was mocked, isolated, and — in the passage just before this one — told by God that he would not be allowed to marry, as a sign to a doomed nation. By the time we reach chapter 15 he is complaining to God about his birth.

And then, in the middle of the complaint, this: "Thy words were found, and I did eat them; and thy word was unto me the joy and rejoicing of mine heart: for I am called by thy name, O LORD God of hosts" (Jeremiah 15:16).

He did not merely read them. He ate them. And what he ate became joy in a life that had very little to be joyful about.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **what you feed your inner man on determines what your inner man can carry — and a believer who eats the Word deliberately will stand steady in circumstances that leave a headline-fed believer shaking.**

## Eating Is the Right Image

Notice how physical the metaphor is, and how often Scripture uses it.

"Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). Job says, "I have esteemed the words of his mouth more than my necessary food" (Job 23:12). The psalmist says, "How sweet are thy words unto my taste! yea, sweeter than honey to my mouth!" (Psalm 119:103).

Eating is the right picture because of what eating actually does. Food is not admired; it is taken in, broken down, and made part of the body. Nobody grows by looking at a meal.

**The Word of God** — what He has spoken — is the food. **Renewing of the Mind** — the slow replacement of your thinking with His — is the digestion. **The Life of Faith** — a life that acts on what God says — is the strength that follows.

Consider a man who reads three newspapers before breakfast and one verse before bed, then wonders why his inner weather matches the front page. He is not being dramatic. He is being fed.

### The Two Diets

Everybody eats. The question is never whether you are consuming, but what.

The news is not the enemy and this is not a call to ignorance. Jeremiah was deeply engaged with the disaster around him — he was living in it. The point is proportion and priority, and the honest test is a simple one: what did you take into your mind most this week?

Sit with that for a moment, because most believers would find the answer uncomfortable. Public opinion, commentary, alert after alert, and then a hurried chapter. That is not a failure of devotion. It is a failure of diet, and diets are mostly habit.

Set the two side by side and the effect shows. One man's day is governed by the last thing he read, so his peace rises and falls with the cycle of bad reports. Another reads the same reports, but he has eaten first, and his peace is anchored to something the reports cannot revise. Both are informed. Only one is fed.

### "Thy Words Were Found"

There is a small word in that clause worth noticing: *found*.

Jeremiah did not inherit them casually. He found them — the way a man finds something he has been searching for, and then treats the finding as treasure. There is effort implied, and appetite implied.

That is the difference between a Bible on a shelf and a Bible in a man. Both contain the same text. Only one is being eaten.

### They Became Joy in the Wrong Season

Here is the part that makes this passage remarkable.

Jeremiah was not eating the Word in a good season. He was eating it in the middle of national collapse and personal rejection. And what it produced in him was not removal from the pain — he keeps complaining for another thirty chapters — but a joy that refused to leave.

That tells you something about what the Word does. It does not always change the circumstance. It changes what the circumstance is up against.

### The Name on the Package

The reason given for the joy is easy to miss: "for I am called by thy name."

That is the ground of everything else. He was not eating the words of a stranger. He belonged to the One who spoke them, and the food he was eating was his own family's provision.

Which is why the same passage can feed one believer and bore another. The difference is not intelligence. It is whether you arrive as a stranger to the Author or as a child of the house.

### What Happens When the Famine Comes

Everyone will face a season when the usual supports are gone — when the advisers are confused, the reports are bad, and the inner reserves are thin.

In that hour you will not rise to the level of what you intended to read. You will fall to the level of what you have eaten. What will there be to draw on when the reserves are thin? That is the argument for the daily habit — not legalism, but provision.

## What This Means for You

**1. Take an honest inventory.** Spend two minutes estimating what you consumed this week — hours of news and feeds versus minutes of Scripture. Write both numbers down.

**2. Eat before you scroll.** Make the Word the first thing you take in, not the last thing you squeeze in.

**3. Chew, do not swallow.** Eat one passage slowly and ask what it means before you ask what it feels like.

**4. Set limits on the noise, honestly.** You do not need to be uninformed. You do need to be fed, and the two compete for the same stomach.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the Word is real food. Wisdom says that also means you keep read broadly, use teachers and study helps, stay engaged with the world you are sent into, and do not confuse reading with eating — a person who reads nine chapters and applies none has fasted in a crowded room.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Jeremiah 15:15–21**, and note what God says back to the man who complained.
- **Eat first each morning this week** — a chapter before a headline.
- **Write down one verse a day and carry it**, so the day is fed rather than flooded.
- **Read Matthew 4:1–4**, and count how Jesus answered the enemy.
