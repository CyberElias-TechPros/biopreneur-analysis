Paul's shortest instruction on prayer contains three parts, and the third one is easy to overlook.

"Continue in prayer, and watch in the same with thanksgiving" (Colossians 4:2).

Continue. Watch. Thanksgiving. Three words for a whole life of prayer.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **thanksgiving is not the polite ending of a prayer but the switch that turns a request into trust — it declares that the outcome is already in good hands before the answer is visible.**

## The Three Words

**"Continue"** is *proskartereite* — to persist, to attend constantly, to be devoted to. It is the word used of the disciples' devotion in Acts 2:42. Not a burst of prayer, but a habit.

**"Watch"** is *grēgoreō* — to stay awake, to be alert. It is a military word, used of a guard who must not sleep.

And **"thanksgiving"** is *eucharistia* — gratitude, the giving of thanks.

The three are joined: **The Life of Faith** — persisting in prayer when nothing has changed — is what "continue" requires. **Communion With the Holy Spirit** — the attentiveness of "watch" — keeps a person awake to what God is doing. And **Confession of the Word** — saying what is true about Him — is what thanksgiving is.

Consider a man who sends an important request to someone he trusts and then spends the week checking his phone. His behaviour reports that he does not trust the recipient, whatever he says.

### Thanksgiving Inside the Request

Philippians 4 makes the structure unmistakable: "but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God" (Philippians 4:6).

Read the order — prayer, supplication, *with thanksgiving*, requests. The thanksgiving is enclosed inside the asking. It is not a closing courtesy after the list.

That placement is the instruction. Paul is telling you to thank God for what He has already done and already is *while you are still asking*.

### Why That Works

There is nothing magical about the words. The mechanism is what gratitude does to the person praying.

Gratitude forces you to remember. It is difficult to be simultaneously convinced that God has done nothing for you and to list what He has done.

And gratitude relocates the centre. A request made in a vacuum is a demand. A request made inside gratitude is a child asking a father who has already been generous.

Set the two prayers side by side. One is entirely about a gap — what is missing, urgent, unfixed. The other names the gap but begins by describing the provider. Same request. Two different rooms.

### The One Who Came Back

Luke 17 gives the uncomfortable illustration: ten lepers healed, one returned. "And Jesus answering said, Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine?" (Luke 17:17).

The nine were not refused. They were cleansed. Nothing suggests their healing was reversed.

But only one heard "thy faith hath made thee whole" (Luke 17:19) — a fuller word than the healing itself. Gratitude took him further into the blessing.

### The Unreasonable Command

First Thessalonians takes it to a place that sounds almost impossible: "In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you" (1 Thessalonians 5:18).

*In* everything, not *for* everything. That distinction is the whole article in one preposition. You are not asked to thank God for the diagnosis. You are asked to thank Him in the middle of the week the diagnosis arrived.

That is possible because thanksgiving is directed at a person rather than at a circumstance.

### Thanksgiving as Watchfulness

Colossians joins the two: "watch in the same with thanksgiving."

That pairing is deliberate. A watching person notices. Gratitude is largely the discipline of noticing — the provision that arrived, the prayer answered six months ago, the ordinary mercies that go unnoticed because they are ordinary.

An unthankful person is not usually ungrateful by decision. He is simply unobservant.

### Making It a Practice

**Make a list before you ask.** Three things, named specifically.

**Thank Him for the answer not yet seen** — not as a claim that it has happened, but as confidence in the One who promised.

**Keep a record.** A note of answers becomes a document you can read in the next dry season.

**Say it out loud.** Thanksgiving spoken is louder inside you than thanksgiving thought.

### The Honest Boundary

For anyone in genuine grief, one thing must be said clearly.

Thanking God in a hard season is not a requirement to feel positive. The psalms model something more honest: praise and lament in the same breath — "I will say unto God my rock, Why hast thou forgotten me?" (Psalm 42:9) appears in a psalm that also says "Hope thou in God."

Gratitude and grief are not opposites. Both are spoken to the same person.

## What This Means for You

**1. Put thanks first.** Before the list, name three things.

**2. Thank Him for the outcome you cannot see.** Let the confidence be in His character.

**3. Watch.** Notice what has already been given this week.

**4. Keep a record of answers.** Future you will need it.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says thanksgiving is the atmosphere of trusting prayer. Wisdom says it is not a substitute for the request, and that a person in deep sorrow may need the lament form first — which Scripture also provides.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Colossians 4:2–6** daily this week.
- **Begin every prayer with three specific thanks.**
- **Read Luke 17:11–19**, and note the question Jesus asked.
- **Start a page** where you record answers as they come.
