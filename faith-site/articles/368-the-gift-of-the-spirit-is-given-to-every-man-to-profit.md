A woman sits in a congregation for years, convinced that spiritual gifts belong to the people on the platform. She is faithful, she is sincere, and she has quietly concluded that the striking words in 1 Corinthians were written about someone else. Read the verse she has been skimming and the conclusion collapses in one sentence.

"But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to every man to profit withal" (1 Corinthians 12:7). Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the Spirit's gifts are not awards for the impressive; they are equipment issued to every believer with a stated purpose — the profit of others — which means no one is excluded and no one is a spectator.**

## Three Words That Settle the Question

Paul makes his case with three ordinary words, and each one closes a door that insecurity had opened.

**Manifestation.** The Greek is *phanerosis* — a making-visible. A manifestation is not a private impression that stays inside you; it is something that becomes evident. Paul is not describing a rumour of the Spirit but a demonstration of Him. When the gift operates, the room can tell.

**Given.** This is the word that ends the argument about qualifications. In the same chapter Paul writes, "But all these worketh that one and the selfsame Spirit, dividing to every man severally as he will" (1 Corinthians 12:11). The distribution belongs to the Spirit's will, not your résumé. **A gift is not a reward for maturity; it is equipment for assignment.** That is why Scripture says "every man" without adding a minimum age, income, education, or temperament.

**Profit.** The Greek is *sumphero* — to bring together for advantage, to be of benefit. It is a marketplace word, and it is deliberately unspiritual-sounding. The Spirit gives with an outcome in view, and the outcome is not your reputation. It is somebody else's advantage.

Put the three together and the sentence reads like an equipment order: something visible, issued to everyone, for the benefit of others. Which of those three words had you quietly deleted?

### What Profit Does Not Mean

**The Anointing** — the Spirit's enabling presence on a life for a task — is not measured by how the gift makes you feel. Paul's word is profit, and profit is accounted for in someone else's ledger.

This should correct two opposite errors. The first is the believer who never steps out because they feel unqualified. But if the gift were a reward for qualification, the Spirit would be guilty of poor distribution. The second is the believer who treats the gift as a personal possession, a way of being noticed. Between those two errors stands the word *profit*, and it settles both.

Notice too what Paul does in the verses that follow. He does not tell the Corinthians to acquire gifts; he tells them the gifts are already distributed and that the body has been arranged. "For as the body is one, and hath many members... so also is Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:12). A member does not apply to become a member. It is already one, and it already has a function.

### The Body Does Not Have Spectators

The human body offers the plainest illustration available, and Paul uses it at length in the same chapter. No sane person argues that a hand is optional. A member that no longer functions is not a humble member; it is a paralysed one, and the whole body feels it.

Here is how this shows up in ordinary church life. A man with the gift of giving has quietly concluded that his contribution is unspiritual because it is not vocal. A teenager with a word of knowledge has been told to wait until he is older. A woman who prays and sees answers, week after week, has never told anyone because she assumed the pattern was a coincidence. In each case the profit that was issued never reached the people it was issued for. That is not humility, is it? It is a distribution bottleneck.

**Peter states the same economics in plainer terms:** "As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God" (1 Peter 4:10). The word is *steward* — a manager of someone else's property. You do not own the gift; you hold it in trust, and the Owner expects it to be working.

### Why the Gifts Come With a Charge

Paul tells Timothy, "Wherefore I put thee in remembrance that thou stir up the gift of God, which is in thee" (2 Timothy 1:6). The Greek for *stir up* is *anazopureo*, to rekindle a fire. Notice the shape of the instruction: Timothy is not told to ask for a gift. He is told to rekindle what is already in him.

That single verse explains a great deal of frustration. Believers wait for what has been issued and never reach for the fire already lit. The stirring is our part; the giving was His. And stirring is not a mystical experience — in Timothy's case it came through the exercise of ministry and the company of faithful people.

Look at the contrast between two congregations. In the first, the gifts are admired from a distance, discussed in doctrine, and exercised by three people. The room is orderly and the body is thin. In the second, ordinary believers risk ordinary words and ordinary acts of service, and the room is messier and the body is fed. **One church admires the equipment. The other uses it.** The Spirit's will is not in doubt; the question is whether the issued gift ever leaves the storeroom.

### Profit Requires Courage, Not Polish

Every gift in 1 Corinthians 12 requires someone to act before they can be sure. A word of wisdom spoken under the check of Scripture and love can be weighed by the hearers; a word never spoken can never be weighed at all. Paul's instruction that "the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets" (1 Corinthians 14:32) assumes that the gift operates in a human being, with self-control, and that the congregation tests what is said.

That is the balance the New Testament keeps and ours must keep. The gift is real and needs stepping out on; the gift is not infallible and needs weighing. Boldness without testing is chaos; testing without boldness is a library of unused equipment.

## What This Means for You

**1. Retire the phrase "I have nothing."** It is not humility and it is not in the text. Every man means every man, and if you are in Christ, the manifestation is available to you.

**2. Ask what profit, not what platform.** The Spirit issues gifts for advantage, not applause. Identify one person or situation you could actually serve this week where you currently do nothing.

**3. Take one low-risk step of obedience.** Offer to pray for one person, share one verse you have been carrying, serve one practical need without announcing it. Gifts grow by use, and courage grows with them.

**4. Keep love in the driver's seat.** Paul spends all of chapter 13 before he returns to gifts in chapter 14. A gift without love is noise; a small gift with love is heaven's own arithmetic.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom govern the exercise.** Faith steps out; wisdom keeps the word inside the limits of Scripture, love, and good order. Faith without wisdom turns a gift into a spectacle. Wisdom without faith keeps the equipment in the box. Neither replaces prayer, honest counsel, or the plain work of serving people well.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 1 Corinthians 12 in one sitting** and underline every occurrence of "every," "all," and "each."
- **Write down one gift** you sense has been at work in you, however quietly, and one person who has been profited by it.
- **Act once this week** on the plainest opportunity in front of you and note what happened.
- **Ask a mature believer** to tell you honestly what they see in you, and let their answer correct your self-assessment.
- **Read 1 Corinthians 13:1–3** and hold it beside your step of faith before you take it.
