There is a difference between a house that has been visited and a house that has been moved into.

Paul uses the language of residence in Colossians 3:16: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom; teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the Lord."

Dwell. Not stop by. Not drop in when things go wrong.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the Word is meant to move in and stay — and where it is given a residency rather than a visit, it produces wisdom, conversation, and song that no amount of information can manufacture.**

## The Word *Enoikeō*

The Greek verb is *enoikeō* — to dwell in, to inhabit, to make a home in. It is the same word family Paul uses when he says the Spirit of God dwells in you (Romans 8:9).

And it is a present imperative: keep on letting it dwell. Not a one-time decision but a standing permission, renewed.

Then the adverb: *plousiōs*, richly, abundantly, copiously. The word is about wealth, not adequacy. Not "enough of the Word to get by," but a house so full of it that it spills into every room.

**The Word of God** — what He has spoken — is the resident. **Spiritual Understanding** — the capacity to see what God is saying — is what makes room for it. **Communion With the Holy Spirit** — the ongoing conversation with the One who wrote it — is the atmosphere of the house.

Consider two homes on the same street. One has a visitor's coat on a hook by the door. The other has books on the shelves, music in the kitchen, and children who know the stories by heart. The second house says something the first cannot.

### "Richly in All Wisdom"

The phrase in the middle is easy to slide past, and it is doing real work: *en pasē sophia* — in all wisdom.

The Word dwelling richly does not produce a person with a large vocabulary of verses. It produces a person with wisdom — the ability to apply truth to a situation that no verse addresses directly.

That is a crucial distinction. A concordance can tell you what the Bible says. Wisdom tells you what to do on a Tuesday afternoon when your colleague is lying about you and no verse covers the exact case.

Set the two side by side. One believer has memorised a great deal and cannot decide anything without a proof text. Another has let the Word live in him long enough that its logic has become his instinct, and he knows what to do. The first has storage. The second has wisdom.

### The Overflow: Teaching and Admonishing

Now notice what follows, because this is where the verse turns outward.

Where the Word dwells richly, two things appear in a person's conversation: teaching and admonishing. Teaching is building someone up in what is true. Admonishing is putting a hand on a shoulder and saying the needed thing, gently.

Both are *mutual* in this verse — "one another." This is not a clergy function. It is what happens when ordinary believers are full of the Word: they become useful to each other.

And the instrument is striking: "psalms and hymns and spiritual songs." The Word at home in a person becomes singable. Truth that has been digested has a melody to it, because it has reached the affections and not only the intellect.

### Song as Evidence

That is worth pausing on, because it is a diagnostic.

A person can recite doctrine under pressure and still be joyless. A person in whom the Word is dwelling richly tends to sing — not because his circumstances are pleasant, but because there is something in the house that is bigger than the circumstances.

Paul and Silas sang in a prison at midnight (Acts 16:25). That is not a strategy. That is a full house.

### The Deuteronomy Pattern

This is not a new idea. Moses had already described it: "And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up" (Deuteronomy 6:6–7).

Notice where the words are meant to live: in the house, on the road, at bedtime, at breakfast. Not in a compartment marked "religion."

That is what dwelling means. It is not intensity for an hour. It is presence across the day.

### How a House Gets Occupied

The practical question, then, is how the residency actually happens. The verse says "let."

That verb puts the initiative with you. It is not a passive hope that the Word will somehow seep in. It is a standing decision about what has access to the rooms of your mind — what plays in the car, what sits on the table, what you talk about at dinner, what you reach for in the gap between two tasks.

Small permissions, repeated, become occupancy. Which room have you kept locked?

## What This Means for You

**1. Change what plays in the background.** What fills the silence in your day? Give the Word a share of the audio, the shelf, and the wall.

**2. Aim at wisdom, not volume.** When you read, ask what this teaches you about how to act, not only what it teaches you to affirm.

**3. Talk about it casually.** Deuteronomy puts the words in ordinary conversation, not only in formal settings. Let a verse come up at dinner.

**4. Sing something.** If the Word is dwelling richly, a song will surface. Sing it deliberately if it does not come naturally.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the Word at home in you will produce wisdom and song. Wisdom says that filling a house is a matter of pacing and seasons — a grieving believer may have one verse on the wall for a year, and that is a full house — and that "richly" is measured by depth rather than by the number of chapters logged.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Colossians 3:12–17 slowly**, and note how many things follow from the Word dwelling.
- **Choose one place in your home** where a verse will live this week.
- **Put the Word in the car or the kitchen** — audio, a card, a chorus.
- **Speak a verse into someone's life** this week, gently and without a lecture.
