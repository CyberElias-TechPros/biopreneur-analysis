Seventy men came back from a preaching tour and reported something they had not expected: "Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name" (Luke 10:17). They had been sent to heal the sick and announce the kingdom. What startled them was not that the sick recovered, but that the darkness obeyed.

Jesus answered with a commission and a caution, in that order.

"Behold, I give unto you power to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy: and nothing shall by any means hurt you" (Luke 10:19). Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the commission to tread on serpents is real, delegated, and narrower than we assume — it covers the enemy's power, not every ordinary hazard, and it always points back to a name written in heaven.**

## Two Words, One Commission

The verse carries two different Greek words, and the difference matters more than any sermon outline.

The first is *exousia* — authority, the right to act. The second is *dunamis* — power, the ability to act. Jesus says He gives you *exousia* over all the *dunamis* of the enemy. The right is given; the force is the enemy's. You are not being handed the enemy's strength. You are being handed jurisdiction over it.

**The Authority of the Believer** — the God-given right and ability, in Christ, to stand and act in His name — is the *exousia*. **The Name of Jesus** — His person, work, and authority — is the jurisdiction. **Spiritual Warfare** — the conflict over what you will believe and say — is where that jurisdiction is exercised.

Consider a courtroom bailiff. He is not stronger than the defendant across the room, and he would lose a wrestling match badly. He does not need to be stronger. He has a court's commission, and the defendant's strength is not the relevant question. Authority always works like this: it functions because of who issued it, not because of the muscle of the one carrying it.

### Serpents, Scorpions, and What They Represent

In the Old Testament, serpents carry a long and specific association with the enemy's work. The serpent in Eden deceives (Genesis 3:1). The serpents in the wilderness are the instruments of a judgment that a bronze serpent was lifted to heal (Numbers 21:6–9). The psalm the early church would have known puts the image next to deliverance: "thou shalt tread upon the lion and adder: the young lion and the dragon shalt thou trample under feet" (Psalm 91:13).

Scorpions add a different note. A scorpion does not kill as quickly as a viper; it stings, and the pain lingers. In Scripture a scorpion is the image of something that hurts without destroying — "the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (Ezekiel 18:2) is that kind of pain. Some opposition is sharp and quick. Much of it stings and lingers.

Jesus is not merely describing the animals a barefoot traveller might meet on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. He is naming the powers standing behind them. The prayer book of Israel already treated that psalm as a promise about protection from the enemy's ambush, and Jesus now puts the whole category under the feet of the seventy.

### The Scope That Keeps This Honest

Here is where teaching on this verse frequently goes wrong. "Nothing shall by any means hurt you" has been turned into a promise that no believer will ever be in a car accident, bitten by a snake, or sick in a hospital. That is not what Luke records, and Luke of all writers would have known it.

Read Psalm 91 carefully and notice that it is written as a psalm of trust that describes a person who "dwellest in the secret place" — a promise of divine protection, not a policy of insurance. Then read the gospel accounts. Stephen was stoned. James was killed by Herod (Acts 12:2). Paul was shipwrecked, beaten, and bitten by a viper in Acts 28 — and, notably, simply shook it off. The commission was operating in that moment, and it did not take the form of never encountering the snake.

What Jesus is promising is that the enemy's authority cannot finally injure what you are. The treading is real; the durability is what is being guaranteed. Faith is not a refusal to see a doctor, and it does not test God by handling snakes on purpose. Faith uses the authority God gave and the ordinary prudence God expects, and it does not presume a promise He did not make.

### The Joy He Redirects

The seventy had a dangerous reaction available to them, and most of us have taken it at some point. Working power can become the centre of gravity. The next thing Jesus says is the corrective: "Notwithstanding in this rejoice not, that the spirits are subject unto you; but rather rejoice, because your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20).

He does not withdraw the authority. He reorders the joy.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. The minister whose joy rests on results is at the mercy of every result; if the deliverance happens, he is ecstatic, and if nothing moves this week, he is undone. The minister whose joy rests on a written name is steady in both. One uses authority as evidence of importance; the other uses it as evidence of assignment.

There is one more detail worth sitting with. Jesus had already told them the harvest was great and the labourers few (Luke 10:2), and that they were being sent as lambs among wolves. The authority of Luke 10:19 is not given to people who sit in a place of strength. It is given to lambs walking into the wolves' territory. The commission is precisely what makes that sending coherent.

## What This Means for You

**1. Speak to the power, not to the person.** The whole verse is framed around the enemy and his works, and Jesus never once teaches us to attack human beings who oppose us. A colleague who ridicules your faith is not the serpent; he is a person in the serpent's territory. Refuse the tone of contempt — it belongs to the defeat, not the victory.

**2. Use authority as assignment, not as a demonstration.** If the temptation to "prove" your authority has ever entered your prayer life, remember what Jesus said next. Are you more excited about the enemy being subject than about your name being written? That question alone will save you from a very common ministry accident.

**3. Do not invent hazards to test the promise.** Have you ever been tempted to turn a promise of protection into a dare?  Faith is not presumption, and a believer who deliberately handles danger to prove the verse is doing what the Lord declined to do in the wilderness (Luke 4:12). Use the authority you have been given over the real opposition in your actual life, and take sensible care of the body God gave you.

**4. Keep the practical channels open alongside the spiritual one.** When a doctor says something frightening, tread on the fear, resist the diagnosis's claim to speak a final verdict, and still take the medication. Faith and Wisdom were never in competition: faith contends for the verdict, wisdom manages the journey, and both belong to the same Father.

**5. Rejoice in the thing that cannot be revoked.** Healings can vary, seasons can shift, and results can be confusing. A name written in heaven is not on that sliding scale. Anchor your joy where the enemy has no jurisdiction at all.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Luke 10:1–24 in one sitting** and note every time Jesus mentions what the disciples should not be excited about.
- **Name the "scorpion" in your life** — the opposition that stings and lingers rather than breaking you — and address it in prayer by name.
- **Write Luke 10:20 out and carry it**, which is what the Lord told them to rejoice in.
- **Do one deliberate act of ordinary prudence this week** in the area where you have been only declaring and not doing.
