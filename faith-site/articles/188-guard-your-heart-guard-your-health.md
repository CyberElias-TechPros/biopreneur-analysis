Most people guard their health with their habits and never think about the one thing Proverbs says is upstream of all of them.

"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life" (Proverbs 4:23).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the heart is not the private room where nothing shows; it is the spring of the body's life, and what you let settle there eventually shows up in your flesh.**

## The Word and the Guard

### "Keep" and "Diligence"

The verb is *nāṣar* — to guard, to watch over, to keep as a sentry keeps a post. And the noun translated "diligence" is *mišmār*, a watch or a guard-post.

So the instruction is military rather than sentimental: **post a guard at the gate of your heart, because that gate is the one the whole city depends on.**

And the word for heart is *lēb*, which in Hebrew is not the emotional centre only. It is the seat of thought, intention, will, and decision — the inward person.

### The Spring and the Issues

The reason given is the most important part of the verse: "for out of it are the issues of life."

"Issues" is *tôṣāʾôṯ* — goings-out, outgoings, the source of a river's streams. The same word family appears for the springs of water.

That is a hydraulic image, not a psychological one. The heart is a spring, not a warehouse. What comes out of your life is drawn from what is in the spring.

Which means the visible failures are downstream reports about an upstream condition. The angry outburst, the collapse under pressure, the illness that arrives in a season of grief — these are streams. The spring is where the guarding happens.

### The Body and the Heart

And Proverbs does not leave the connection in metaphor. Look at how often the same book ties the inward state to the physical frame.

"A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones" (Proverbs 17:22).

Read the first half as it stands in the Hebrew — a joyful heart produces good healing, and the word for medicine is *gēhâ*, a remedy. And the word for "doeth good" is *yāṭab*, to make well.

Now read the second half. "A broken spirit drieth the bones." Bones are the deepest, driest part of the body. This is not a poetic decoration; it is a description of a person who has been worn down from the inside.

"The spirit of a man will sustain his infirmity; but a wounded spirit who can bear it?" (Proverbs 18:14). Both truths in one verse. A sustained spirit carries a body; a wounded one collapses the house.

### What the New Testament Adds

The New Testament picks up the same physiology and puts it in the church's mouth.

"Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath" (Ephesians 4:26). Then the list of things to put away: "bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking... with all malice" (Ephesians 4:31).

And the reason given, in the same passage: "And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption" (Ephesians 4:30).

And Hebrews adds the physical image: "Looking diligently lest any man fail of the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled" (Hebrews 12:15).

**A root is under the ground.** Its first appearance is a fruit, and by then the work of pulling it up is much harder.

### The Bitterness Question

This needs saying plainly, because it is where the teaching has been handled badly and where it is most useful.

Resentment is not a harmless private feeling. It is a persistent, low-grade physiological event. Unforgiveness keeps the body in a state that has a name in ordinary conversation — being wound up, being on edge — and no body can live there indefinitely without cost.

That is not a claim that every illness is caused by a grudged grievance. See the last article but one on where sickness comes from; the assignment of blame is rarely ours to make. But it is a claim that Scripture treats the inward life and the body as one, and that we often treat them as two separate addresses.

### David and the Kept Silence

David gave the condition a clinical account when he finally wrote about it.

"When I kept silence, my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long. For day and night thy hand was heavy upon me: my moisture is turned into the drought of summer" (Psalm 32:3–4).

Look at the symptoms he names. Bones aging. Moisture drying. A roaring inside that went on all day. That is a man describing the wear on a body from carrying a hidden thing.

And then the relief, in the next verse: "I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid" (Psalm 32:5).

The remedy was not a supplement. It was the opening of the mouth. He had been guarding the wrong thing.

### What the Guard Is Not

Three things the guard is not, because "guard your heart" is easily turned into a personality disorder.

**It is not suspicion.** Guarding the heart is not assuming everyone will hurt you. A sentry who suspects every traveller is not defending a city; he is starving it.

**It is not isolation.** A guarded heart is not a closed heart. You cannot both command a fortress and refuse the people God sends to help.

**And it is not denial.** Pushing a feeling into the basement is not governing it. The basement floods.

What the guard is: **a deliberate stewardship of what gets in and what is refused**. You do not control what happens to you. You do control what you allow to stay.

### The Filter

One illustration, and it is the one that makes this practical.

A house has a water filter on the main line. It does not stop the water; it stops the sediment. And nobody says the filter is "in denial about the water."

That is the difference between a guard and a wall. A wall keeps everything out. A filter lets the water through and stops what would damage the pipes.

A guarded heart does this with everything that arrives:

- It lets the criticism through but stops the accusation.
- It lets the news through but stops the fear.
- It lets the memory through but stops the verdict.

The verse says the issues of life come out of the heart. **You are not deciding whether anything comes out — you are deciding what the spring tastes like.**

So what has been coming out of yours lately?

### How to Keep the Spring

So the practice, concretely.

**Confess quickly.** Not because God is keeping a record, but because held guilt is a physical burden. Psalm 32 is the psalm of a man who discovered that.

**Forgive before the sun sets.** Paul's instruction on the timing of anger is not arbitrary courtesy. He puts it in a letter about unity and a letter about the Spirit's indwelling.

**Renew the mind deliberately.** The **Renewing of the Mind** is the New Testament's word for what Proverbs calls guarding the spring. It happens by the Word, taken in on purpose, and it happens slowly.

**Choose the treatment of your inward condition, not only the symptom.** A **Consciousness of Righteousness** is the most health-giving disposition there is: not "I have to earn God's kindness," but "I am already accepted." Guilt is a corrosive climate to live in.

And the honest wisdom line: this teaching is not a replacement for medical care. Faith and wisdom work together. If a body is unwell, see a doctor — and also look at the spring. **The two are not competing diagnoses; they are the same person.**

## What This Means for You

**1. Find the spring.** Whatever is showing up in your body or your conduct this week, ask what is upstream of it.

**2. Name one thing you have let settle.** There is usually one. Say it out loud to God, and to someone who can hear it.

**3. Install the filter.** Decide this week what you will allow to stay after the news, the mail, or the conversation arrives.

**4. Forgive for the temple.** Not as a favour to the person who wronged you. As maintenance on your own house.

**5. Use the medical means too.** A guarded heart and a kept appointment are the same stewardship, and **Divine Wholeness** includes both.

- **Write your last anger down**
- **Read Psalm 32:1–7** and mark the word "silence."
- **Forgive one person aloud**
