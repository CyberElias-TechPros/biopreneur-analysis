Two blind men followed Jesus crying for mercy. He asked them one question — "Believe ye that I am able to do this?" — and they said yes. Then He touched their eyes and said, "According to your faith be it unto you" (Matthew 9:29).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **Jesus measures outcomes by faith rather than by feeling, which means your emotional weather is a report about your nervous system, not a verdict about your future.**

## The Measure Jesus Named

Three times in Matthew's Gospel the phrase recurs — to the two blind men, to the woman with the issue of blood ("thy faith hath made thee whole," Matthew 9:22), and to the centurion's servant. It is not a formula; it is a pattern. Jesus keeps directing attention away from the intensity of the encounter and onto the confidence of the person.

**The Life of Faith** — a life ordered by God's declarations — has a different measurement system from the one we inherit by default. **The Word of God** — His settled utterance — is the standard. And **Renewing of the Mind** — the gradual replacement of your thought patterns with God's — is how the two finally line up.

Notice that Jesus never asked the blind men how they felt. He asked what they believed. That is not because feelings are wicked; it is because feelings were never designed to be the arbiter.

### Where Feelings Actually Belong

Feelings are real, informative, and usually honest about the wrong thing. Anxiety tells you your body has detected a threat; it does not tell you whether the threat is real. Sadness tells you something matters; it does not tell you how matters will end.

A car's warning light is useful. You would be foolish to cover it, and more foolish to navigate by it. Feelings are the dashboard, not the road.

The line between them runs here: The feeling-led believer asks "do I feel believed?" and waits; the faith-led believer asks "what has God said?" and acts. One consults a gauge; the other reads a map.

Hebrews makes the same distinction with unusual bluntness. Moses "endured, as seeing him who is invisible" (Hebrews 11:27) — not as feeling him, and not as seeing the outcome. The whole cloud of witnesses is introduced that way: people who acted on what they had been told rather than on what their senses reported. If faith were a feeling, the eleventh chapter of Hebrews would be a catalogue of moods. Instead it is a catalogue of decisions — journeys taken, houses left, seas crossed, promises embraced before they were received.

### What the Woman With the Blood Issue Shows

Mark's account gives us the richest case. She had suffered twelve years, spent everything, and grew worse. Her feeling was of exhaustion and shame. Yet she pressed through the crowd and touched the hem of His garment.

Jesus stopped the whole procession to say, "Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole" (Mark 5:34). He credited the faith, not the sensation. She felt power leave Him; she did not feel her own healing first. The testimony followed the touch.

Have you been waiting to feel different before you dare to believe? What if the order in Scripture is always the reverse — believe, then feel — and never the other way round?

One caution is worth stating plainly, because this truth has been abused. Telling a grieving person to stop feeling is not faith; it is cruelty dressed as doctrine. Jesus wept at a graveside He was about to empty. Faith does not hurry grief along — it simply refuses to let grief have the final word.

### Feelings Are Honest About the Wrong Thing

The woman with the issue of blood had twelve years of evidence that her body was not healed: "and had suffered many things of many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered, but rather grew worse" (Mark 5:26). That is a medical record, and it was accurate.

She pressed through the crowd anyway. "For she said, If I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole" (Mark 5:28). Mark records her reasoning — she said it, presumably to herself, in the middle of a crowd, with no evidence to support it. Her confession contradicted her chart.

Jesus's response did not rebuke the contradiction: "Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague" (Mark 5:34). He named the faith, not the feeling.

## What This Means for You

"Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God" (Psalm 42:5). The psalmist does not deny the depression. He talks to it. He addresses the soul as one addresses a frightened child, and then answers his own question with hope.

That is the practical shape of living by faith over feeling. You do not wait for calm; you speak to the thing that is not calm.

**1. Ask the question Jesus asked.** "Believe ye that I am able?" Answer it honestly, not emotionally.
**2. Give feelings their seat, not their throne.** Listen, then decide.
**3. Do the next faithful thing.** Action taken in obedience outruns analysis of mood.
**4. Feed the deciding faculty.** Faith comes by hearing, so read and hear more than you scroll.
**5. Faith and wisdom read the body honestly.** Persistent anxiety or despair can carry clinical weight; wisdom seeks help and rest while faith still holds the promise.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Name your feelings once a day** without letting them name you.
- **Write Matthew 9:29** where you will see it.
- **Take one action** you have been postponing until you "felt ready."
- **Speak to your soul** with Psalm 42:5 each morning this week.
