Jesus says something in Matthew 18 that is easy to read too quickly.

"Woe unto the world because of offences! for it must needs be that offences come; but woe to that man by whom the offence cometh!" (Matthew 18:7).

Two halves. In the first, He says offenses are inevitable. In the second, He says the one who causes them is in trouble.

Notice what He does not say. He does not say "blessed are the offenses." He does not say being hurt does not matter. He says both things are true: you will be wounded, and the wounding is serious business with God.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **offenses are inevitable but not sovereign — and refusing to become the confidant of bitterness is one of the most decisive exercises of spiritual authority available to a believer.**

## Two Words That Are Not the Same

The New Testament uses one word family for both the event and the response, and English separates them cleanly while the Bible does not.

The Greek word *skandalon* is the trap, the stumbling stone, the thing that trips you up. That is an offense in the first sense — the wounding event. It is what someone does to you.

Bitterness is different. Hebrews calls it a "root of bitterness" (Hebrews 12:15) and warns that it springs up, troubles the whole person, and defiles many. That is an offense in the second sense — the wounding that has been agreed to and housed.

**The Authority of the Believer** — the right to stand in His name — is exercised here more often than in any dramatic setting. **Spiritual Warfare** — the conflict over what you will believe and say — decides what happens after the wound. **Righteousness** — your settled standing in Christ — is the reason you can afford to release a debt.

Consider a tenant who is genuinely wronged by a landlord, then stops paying rent out of grievance and slowly loses the flat. The grievance was justified. The response forfeited the home. That is what offense does when it is allowed to become a policy.

### Why Offenses Are Inevitable

Part of the reason is that people are not careful. Jesus speaks about a millstone around the neck for those who harm "one of these little ones" (Matthew 18:6), which tells you how seriously He regards the damage.

But there is a less comfortable part of the reason, and Jesus implies it right there. "It must needs be." Betrayal, misunderstanding, unfairness, and neglect are structural to life in a fallen world. They will arrive.

So the question a believer eventually faces is not "will I be wounded?" but "what will I do with the wound?" Offense-proof living is not a life without wounds. It is a life in which the wounds do not get a vote after the fact.

### The Trap in the Word

The word *skandalon* was used of the bait-stick in an animal trap. That image is worth holding onto, because it names the mechanism precisely.

A trap works because the animal cannot be anywhere else but in the trap. The little stick does not defeat the animal; it holds it while the net falls.

Offense works like this. A person says something cutting, and the comment is the stick. The net is whatever you do next — the rehearsal, the silence, the story you tell three other people, the relationship you quietly write off. The comment did not wound you deeply; the net is what did the lasting damage.

That means the authority in this teaching is not over the stick. It is over the net.

### What Forgiveness Is Not

Before we talk about how, we need to remove the errors, because forgiveness has been badly taught.

Forgiveness does not mean the harm did not happen. It does not mean the person was right. It does not restore trust automatically, and it does not require you to remain in an unsafe situation.

Jesus does not tell you to pretend. He tells you to release. There is a great difference between saying "it does not matter" and saying "I will not be your debtor's keeper."

And here is the sentence that needs to be heard carefully: forgiveness is not a feeling. It is a decision you may have to make more than once, and often with your fists clenched.

### The Reason a Wound Returns

Many sincere believers have forgiven a person and then found the whole thing back six months later, and they conclude the forgiveness did not take.

It did take. What returned was not the un-forgiveness; it was the memory. The decision stands, and the memory needs the decision applied again. That is what Paul means by allowing no "gap" for the enemy (2 Corinthians 2:11) — a small opening, not a repeated fall.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. One believer assumes that a returning memory means the forgiveness failed, and so feels the whole wound again as evidence of a fresh failure. Another understands that the decision is a standing choice and re-applies it whenever the memory surfaces. The first relives; the second rules.

### The Authority You Forfeit

Here is the part almost nobody says, and it belongs in an article about authority.

Bitterness costs you jurisdiction. A believer who is angry with a person becomes unable to pray effectively for them, unable to hear God clearly about them, and — this is the sting — often unable to receive from the people who have hurt them.

Jesus connects this directly in Mark 11. Right after the fig tree, He says, "when ye stand praying, forgive, if ye have ought against any: that your Father also which is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses" (Mark 11:25). The context is a teaching on faith that moves mountains. Forgiveness is not a separate topic inserted here; it is attached to prayer that works.

Which means unforgiveness does not just hurt you relationally. It disarms you in the very place where authority is exercised.

## What This Means for You

**1. Name the offense plainly before you release it.** How long have you been nursing this instead of naming it? Vague forgiveness is usually no forgiveness. Say what happened, say it to God, and then release it.

**2. Forgive as a decision, with a date if helpful.** Feelings follow the decision; they are not the decision. Some believers have found it helpful to write the release down and keep it, so the memory has something to answer to.

**3. Distinguish forgiveness from trust.** You can release a person completely and still decide not to lend them money, leave a child with them, or share a confidence with them. Wisdom is not bitterness.

**4. Deal with the telling.** Much bitterness is kept alive by the rehearing — the story you repeat. The next time your friend hears the third retelling, you may be feeding the root rather than the relationship.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says what God says: you are forgiven, and you can forgive. Wisdom says what God says as well: guard the situation, keep the boundary, and get help where there has been real harm. Faith does not require trauma to be ignored, and wisdom does not require enmity to be kept.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 18:1–35 in one sitting**, and notice the story Jesus attached to His teaching on offenses.
- **Write the name of one person you have forgiven but still resent**, and speak the release aloud.
- **Withdraw the rehearing.** Note how often you tell this story, and stop.
- **Read Hebrews 12:12–17** and consider what the root produces in the person who keeps it.
