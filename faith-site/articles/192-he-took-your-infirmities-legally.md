A man came to Jesus with a body that had stopped cooperating, and Jesus did not ask him how strong his faith was. He touched him, and the leprosy was gone. Then He did it again, and again, and again, until the evening of that day looked like a hospital emptying out.

**Matthew 8:17** looks at that evening and tells us what the Holy Spirit wants us to see in it: "That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses."

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **healing is not a favour God may be persuaded to grant; it is a debt Christ already discharged in His own body, and the gospel simply tells you where the receipt is kept.**

Most believers pray as though healing has to be earned by a good week, then wonder why their prayers feel like negotiations they keep losing. But a debt is not earned; it is settled or it is not. Either He took your infirmities or He did not.

## The Word Matthew Chose

### What "Took" Actually Means

Matthew is quoting **Isaiah 53:4**, and the Hebrew verb is *nasa* — to lift, to carry, to bear away. It is the word a man uses when he picks up a load and puts it on his own shoulders, and it is the same word the law uses for a substitute taking the guilt of another.

So the picture is not sympathy. It is transfer. Your sickness was picked up and moved to another address.

Matthew then adds a second word: "bare our sicknesses." The Greek is *ebastasen*, from *bastazō* — to carry like freight, like a burden borne on the back rather than held in the hands. Two verbs, both speaking of weight, both pointing away from you.

**The burden moved. That is the whole of it.**

### The Word the Translators Softened

Here is a detail most readers never hear, and it matters.

The Hebrew of Isaiah 53:4 says the Servant bore our *choli* — our sickness — and our *makob* — our pain. Some English versions render these as "griefs" and "sorrows," which is not wrong, but it can leave a reader thinking the verse is only about emotional weight.

Matthew settles the question. Under the inspiration of the Spirit, the apostle takes that exact Hebrew pair and translates them into the plain Greek words for disease and illness — *astheneias* and *nosous*. He is telling us, in a quotation, how the Holy Spirit reads Isaiah.

So when someone says healing is not in the atonement, they are not disagreeing with a preacher's overreach. They are disagreeing with Matthew's translation. And if the Spirit chose those words to describe the cross, what exactly is left to negotiate?

### A Citation That Costs Something

Matthew could have quoted a dozen texts about the Servant. He picked this one.

And notice what was happening in the passage when he quoted it. Jesus had just healed a leper, a centurion's servant at a distance, and Peter's mother-in-law with a fever, and then "they brought unto him many that were possessed with devils: and he cast out the spirits with his word, and healed all that were sick" (Matthew 8:16).

Matthew watched a whole evening of healing and reached for Isaiah 53 to explain it. Which means, in his mind, the cross was already visible in the consultation room. The healing was not a side effect of the visit; it was the will of God arriving early.

### The Tense of the Receipt

Read the verb in Isaiah: "He **hath** borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows" (Isaiah 53:4). Past tense, written six centuries before the cross, because with God the finished work is spoken of as done before it is done.

Then read the verb in 1 Peter: "Who his own self **bare** our sins in his own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24) — and in the same verse, "by whose stripes ye **were** healed."

Peter puts sin and sickness in the same sentence, borne in the same body, on the same tree. If you have accepted the first half of that verse, you have already accepted the grammar of the second.

### What a Legal Transfer Requires

#### Three Things Every Debt Must Have

A debt is not cancelled by wishing. It needs a debtor, a debt, and a payment that is accepted. Look at how the gospel keeps those three in view.

**The debtor.** "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). You are not a neutral party watching a transaction. You are the sheep whose debt was transferred, and **The Finished Work** is what makes the transfer legal rather than sentimental.

**The debt.** Sin and sickness are not twins, but they entered through one door, and Scripture keeps them in view together. Romans 5:12 says death entered by sin, and death in the body wears a thousand everyday faces, sickness among them.

**The payment.** "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13). The law's curse, not merely its penalty. Moses wrote the curse in Deuteronomy 28 and he wrote sickness into it by name (Deuteronomy 28:22, 27–28, 35). If the curse named sickness, and Christ redeemed you from the curse, then the arithmetic is not difficult.

#### What the Payment Does Not Do

Two honest boundaries, because a legal claim can be overstated into a caricature.

It does not make you sick-proof in a fallen world. Paul is the man who wrote half the New Testament and then told Timothy, "use a little wine for thy stomach's sake and thine often infirmities" (1 Timothy 5:23). The atonement is complete and the body is still mortal, and both are true at once.

And it does not make healing automatic, in the sense of requiring nothing of you. A will can be signed and still sit unread in a drawer. **The Inheritance of the Believer** is real whether or not the heir knows it exists — but an heir who never opens the will lives like a stranger to his own house. That is not God withholding; that is a family that has not read the document.

#### Why "Legal" Is Not a Cold Word

Some believers flinch at legal language, as though the cross were a courtroom and not a garden. But look at where the word leads.

A debt settled in full is not a cold fact. It is the reason a man walks out of the courthouse and goes home to his children. **Your Legal Position** — who and where you are before God in Christ — is the ground under your feet, not paperwork filed against you.

Orphans beg for what belongs to them. Children read the will and take possession. Which one are you acting like this week? And when the symptoms return to argue, whose paperwork are you going to read — the accuser's, or the Father's?

## What This Means for You

**1. Stop praying as though the price is still open.** Healing is not a negotiation you can lose on form. You are not persuading a reluctant God; you are agreeing with a finished settlement.

**2. Answer the accusation with the receipt.** When the enemy whispers that this illness is yours to keep, the answer is not a better feeling. It is Matthew 8:17, quoted out loud.

**3. Let the legal fact shape your expectation.** A will does not become true when the heir feels brave. It is true, and the heir's job is to find out what it says and act on it.

**4. Bring the body to the Healer and the doctor to the body.** The same Lord who bore your sickness gave you a mind that studies medicine. Faith and wisdom work together — prayer and treatment, confession and rest, anointing and the appointment you keep. The atonement is not diminished by a prescription; it is expressed through ordinary means.

**5. Read the Servant song slowly this week.** Isaiah 53 is not a poem about suffering in general. It is an itemised settlement, and you are named in it.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 in one sitting**, and mark every first-person plural pronoun — this was done for "us."
- **Say Matthew 8:17 aloud over your body** each morning before you check how you feel.
- **Write out 1 Peter 2:24** and put a line under "were healed."
- **Thank God for the settlement before you ask for anything.**
- **Read Galatians 3:13–14** and note what the redemption from the curse was for.
