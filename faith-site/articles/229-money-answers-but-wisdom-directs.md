There is a sentence in Ecclesiastes that gets quoted in boardrooms and pulpits with equal enthusiasm, and it is almost always quoted alone.

"A feast is made for laughter, and wine maketh merry: but money answereth all things" (Ecclesiastes 10:19).

Read it in context and it sounds different. The Preacher has just been talking about dull axes, unsharpened tools, and the advantage of wisdom — "Wisdom is better than weapons of war" (Ecclesiastes 9:18).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **money is a genuine answer to a real category of problems, and it is a terrible director of a life, which is why Scripture hands it a budget and never a throne.**

## What the Verse Actually Concedes

### Money Really Does Answer

Let us not be pious about this. Money solves problems.

It buys medicine. It pays school fees. It moves a family out of a flood zone. It funds a translation of Scripture into a language that has none. It feeds a widow.

The Preacher is not being cynical; he is being observant. **A person who pretends money does not answer anything will be a poor steward of it when it arrives.**

### The Category It Answers

But notice the category. Money answers *things* — the practical, material, logistical questions of a life.

It does not answer the questions that matter most. It cannot answer guilt. It cannot answer loneliness. It cannot answer the question of what you are for.

**Money is excellent at the second half of a problem and useless at the first.**

### "Answereth" Is Not "Directeth"

And here is the distinction the verse assumes but does not state.

To answer is to respond to a question already asked. To direct is to decide which question gets asked.

Money is very good at responding. It is very bad at deciding. **A life that lets money ask the questions ends up in a well-funded place it never chose.** And which question has been setting your agenda lately?

### Where Wisdom Enters

#### Wisdom Holds the Map

Proverbs puts the two side by side and ranks them without embarrassment: "Receive my instruction, and not silver; and knowledge rather than choice gold. For wisdom is better than rubies; and all the things that may be desired are not to be compared to it" (Proverbs 8:10–11).

That is not a rejection of silver. It is a statement about priority — **the thing that decides where the silver goes is worth more than the silver.**

#### Wisdom Builds the House

And Proverbs 9 opens with a picture: "Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars" (Proverbs 9:1).

Wisdom is not an abstraction floating above practical life. It is a builder. **It is the faculty that turns resources into something that stands.** What are you building with yours?

#### The Axe and the Edge

Ecclesiastes makes the same point with an axe: "If the iron be blunt, and he do not whet the edge, then must he put to more strength: but wisdom is profitable to direct" (Ecclesiastes 10:10).

That is the whole argument in one image. **You can compensate for dull wisdom by spending more money. It is just expensive.**

#### The Wealth That Cannot Buy the Thing You Want

There is another side to this, and it is the reason the Preacher sounds weary all through the book.

He had more money than anyone in his generation, and he tested it: "And whatsoever mine eyes desired I kept not from them... Then I looked on all the works that my hands had wrought, and on the labour that I had laboured to do: and, behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit" (Ecclesiastes 2:10–11).

A man with unlimited funds ran the experiment, and the finding was that money answers every question except the one he was asking.

**You can buy almost anything with money except the reason to want it.**

### The Steward Who Was Commended

And Jesus commends a dishonest manager — not for dishonesty, but for shrewdness: "for the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light" (Luke 16:8).

Then the application: "And I say unto you, Make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness; that, when ye fail, they may receive you into everlasting habitations" (Luke 16:9).

**Money is meant to be used with enough skill that it buys something eternal.** That is wisdom directing.

### The Boundary

One guardrail, and it is essential because this subject attracts both errors.

The first error is the poverty of false spirituality — treating money as dirty and therefore never learning to manage it, which leaves a believer helpless in the very area where the gospel needs competent stewards.

The second is the opposite: treating wealth as verification of wisdom. It is not. "There is a generation whose teeth are as swords" (Proverbs 30:14) and there are rich fools all through Scripture.

**Faith and Wisdom** keep both errors at bay: money is a tool, and it is a tool that must be learned.

## What This Means for You

**1. Let wisdom choose before money speaks.** Ask what is right, then ask what it costs — not the reverse.

**2. Use money to buy direction.** Courses, advisors, training, a good accountant. Spend a little to sharpen the axe.

**3. Refuse the "how much" question first.** When facing a decision, "what can I afford?" is the wrong opening move.

**4. Give wisdom a source.** James says, "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God" (James 1:5). Direction is available on request.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** **Kingdom Stewardship** is exactly this verse in a life: the purse in one hand, the map in the other.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Ecclesiastes 10:10–20** and mark the axe, the serpent, and the money.
- **Read Proverbs 8:10–21** and note what is compared to gold.
- **Take one decision** and ask wisdom first, cost second.
- **Read James 1:5–8** and ask for wisdom specifically.
- **Write down one thing** money has answered for you, and one thing it never could.
