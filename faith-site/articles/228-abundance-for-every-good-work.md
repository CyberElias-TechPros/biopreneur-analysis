People pray for abundance constantly and specify almost nothing. Paul does the opposite: he names the purpose before he names the supply.

"And God is able to make all grace abound toward you; that ye, always having all sufficiency in all things, may abound to every good work" (2 Corinthians 9:8).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the abundance God promises is measured against a work, not against a wish, and a person who has found the work will always find the supply attached to it.**

## Reading the Sentence in Order

### "God Is Able"

The verse begins with ability, not obligation. Paul is not describing a policy; he is describing a capacity.

That matters, because it moves the whole subject out of the realm of entitlement. **You are not owed abundance. You are invited into a supply that exists because God is able.** So which are you actually asking for?

### "All Grace Abound"

The Greek stacks up: *pas* (all), *charis* (grace), *perisseuō* (to overflow, to be left over). Literally, all grace to overflow toward you.

So the supply described is not a stretch-to-cover arrangement. **It is a surplus, and the surplus has a destination.**

### "All Sufficiency in All Things"

*Autarkeia* — self-sufficiency, contentment, enough. It is the same word Paul uses in Philippians 4:11 for being content in any state.

So the promise includes something more valuable than excess: **the settled feeling of having enough.** Abundance without sufficiency is just anxiety with a bigger number.

### "Abound to Every Good Work"

And the sentence lands on the purpose. The overflow is not for display. It is for *every good work*.

**The measure of your supply is the work in front of you, not the lifestyle beside you.** And what is the work in front of you right now?

### Where the Work Comes From

#### Prepared Beforehand

Paul tells the Ephesians that the work precedes the person: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10).

The works are already appointed. You are not inventing a purpose; you are discovering an assignment.

#### The Supply Matched to the Task

Exodus gives the clearest picture in the Old Testament, and it is almost comical in its abundance.

The people gave for the tabernacle until the craftsmen came to Moses and said, "The people bring much more than enough for the service of the work, which the LORD commanded to make" (Exodus 36:5). So Moses had to command them to stop (Exodus 36:6).

When the work was clear, the supply was overwhelming. The bottleneck was never God's willingness; it was the clarity of the assignment.

#### The Work That Is Already Around You

And there is a discipleship proviso, because "good work" is not always dramatic.

"Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world" (James 1:27).

The most common good work is unglamorous and close by. If the supply seems thin, check whether the assignment has been spiritualised into something distant.

#### When the Work Is Unpaid

One honest case deserves naming, because it is where many believers get stuck.

Some good work does not generate income. Caring for an ageing parent, raising children, serving in a church, mentoring a young believer — none of it appears on a payslip, and all of it is described in Scripture as good work.

So the promise here is not a business plan. It is a supply that can come from anywhere: a spouse's income, a friend's generosity, a church's support, an unexpected provision. **God is not limited to paying you through the thing you are doing.**

Paul himself lived this. He made tents so that the gospel would be free (Acts 18:3), and he received from churches that wanted a share in the work (Philippians 4:15). **The supply came from a different direction than the work, and it still came.**

### The Partnership That Preaches

Philippians adds the relational mechanics: "Now ye Philippians know also, that in the beginning of the gospel... no church communicated with me as concerning giving and receiving, but ye only" (Philippians 4:15).

And then the promise that follows: "But my God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19).

That promise is written to people who funded a mission. It is not a general guarantee dropped on an unexamined life.

## What This Means for You

**1. Name the work before you name the amount.** What is the assignment? Write it down.

**2. Pray for sufficiency, not excess.** *Autarkeia* is the rarer gift, and it makes the surplus usable.

**3. Look for the existing assignment.** Most people are already inside a good work and have not recognised it.

**4. Give where a work is actually happening.** Paul's collection went to a real need, in real churches, with real accountability.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** Believe for overflow and route it deliberately. **Kingdom Stewardship** is what abundance looks like when it is aimed.

### A Prayer That Names the Work

Try praying this way for a month:

*Father, here is the work I believe You have given me. Here is what it needs. Supply it, and show me if I have the work wrong.*

That prayer has an answer waiting for it on both sides. **Either the supply comes, or the assignment gets corrected** — and both outcomes are mercy.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 2 Corinthians 9:6–15** every morning this week.
- **Write your assignment** in one sentence.
- **Read Exodus 36:1–7** and note what had to be stopped.
- **Read Ephesians 2:8–10** and mark "before ordained."
- **Pray one specific request** for the work this week, with a number attached.
