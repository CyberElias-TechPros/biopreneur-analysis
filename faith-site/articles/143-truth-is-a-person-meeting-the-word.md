John begins his Gospel with a sentence that Greek philosophy would have found interesting and Jewish readers would have found alarming.

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1).

Then, fourteen verses later, the claim becomes physical: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the Bible's central subject is not a topic but a Person, and reading it rightly means arriving at Him rather than merely at a position — the words are the road, not the destination.**

## The Word Behind "Word"

The Greek is *Logos*. To a Greek thinker, it meant reason, the rational principle that holds the universe together. To a Jewish reader, it echoed the creative word of God in Genesis — "And God said."

John uses the term and then does something neither audience expected. He says the *Logos* became flesh. Not appeared as flesh, not wore flesh as a disguise. Became.

**The Word of God** — His self-expression — turns out to be a Person rather than a proposition. **Revelation Knowledge** — truth made known — is what happens when you meet Him. **The New Creation** — what you become in Him — is the result of that meeting.

Consider a man who studies his wife's letters for years, catalogues them by date, and never once sits down with her. He knows the correspondence. He has missed the relationship.

### "Dwelt Among Us"

The word is *eskēnōsen* — he pitched his tent. It is the verb of the tabernacle, the movable tent where God's presence lived among a travelling people.

That is a deliberate throwback. The glory that filled the tabernacle now has a face. The presence that came down on a tent in the wilderness is now a man in a village.

And then John adds, "we beheld his glory" — the word for an eyewitness beholding, not a doctrine received.

### Why Jesus Said the Scriptures Testify of Him

The most important verse for how to read the Bible is not about the Bible at all. It is Jesus, speaking to men who had devoted their lives to the text.

"Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me. And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life" (John 5:39–40).

That is a devastating sentence, and it is aimed at experts. They searched correctly, they were diligent, they were right that the Scriptures give life — and they would not come.

The punctiliar error is not reading the Bible. It is reading it and stopping short of its subject.

### The Emmaus Correction

Luke 24 gives the same lesson in a gentler form.

Two disciples are walking and grieving. Jesus joins them, and Luke tells us what He did: "beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27).

Not "the things concerning the moral law." Not "the things concerning Israel's future." The things concerning *himself*.

Which means there is a way to read Moses that misses Him — and Christ is the correction.

### The Veil That Lifts

Paul makes the same point from the other side in 2 Corinthians 3: "But even unto this day, when Moses is read, the vail is upon their heart. Nevertheless when it shall turn to the Lord, the vail shall be taken away" (2 Corinthians 3:15–16).

The veil is not a lack of information. It is a turning. And the turning is toward a Person.

### Why This Does Not Downplay Doctrine

A caution is needed here, because "truth is a Person" can be used to excuse sloppy thinking.

It is not a licence to be vague. The same John who wrote that the Word became flesh also wrote three letters full of tests: test the spirits, test the confession, test whether you love the brothers.

Doctrine matters because this Person has said specific things, and loving Him includes caring what He said. But doctrine is the path rather than the destination.

Set the two errors side by side. One believer treats the faith as a set of propositions to defend, and becomes precise and cold. Another treats the faith as a feeling to enjoy, and becomes warm and unmoored. The first has the map and no country. The second has warmth and no map.

### The Test of a Reading

So there is a simple diagnostic for every study session.

Did the reading end in Him? Not merely in new understanding — good as that is — but in the One it was about. Did it leave you trusting Him more, loving Him more, wanting to obey Him?

Paul says the veil lifts when the heart turns to the Lord. That is the direction every page is supposed to point.

### What He Said About Himself

And He was not coy about it. "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me" (John 14:6). "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35). "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12).

When God's Word became flesh, the Word kept using the metaphor of nourishment, light, and life. He is not information about life. He is life.

## What This Means for You

**1. Ask "where is He in this?"** Not as an allegory trick, but as a habit. Every passage points somewhere toward Christ.

**2. Let your study end in prayer.** When understanding comes, turn it into thanks or trust. Otherwise you have studied a letter.

**3. Beware the expert's danger.** Knowledge of the text is not the same as coming to Him. The Pharisees had the first and refused the second.

**4. Read the whole story as one story.** Moses, the prophets, the psalms — all of it has a subject.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says this Book leads to a Person and goes to meet Him. Wisdom says that means you read it in community — because it is possible to be sincerely wrong about what a verse means, and the same Spirit who illuminates has also given you teachers, and brothers and sisters who know the whole counsel of God.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read John 5:36–40**, and sit with the sentence about coming.
- **Read Luke 24:13–35**, and note what changed when they recognised Him.
- **Take one Old Testament passage** and ask where it points.
- **End every reading this week with one sentence of prayer** rather than a note.
