Joshua was handed the hardest job in the Old Testament.

Moses was dead. The people were camped on the wrong side of a flooded river. The land was full of fortified cities and very large men. And the first thing God says to the new leader is not "be strong" — that comes later, three times — but this:

"This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night, that thou mayest observe to do according to all that is written therein: for then thou shalt make thy way prosperous, and then thou shalt have good success" (Joshua 1:8).

Success is attached to meditation. That is worth pausing over, because it is not how most people plan a conquest. What would you do first, if you were given the land?

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **meditation is the slow process by which a sentence moves from the page to the reflexes — and the believer who chews the Word long enough stops needing to consult it before obeying it.**

## The Word in the Mouth

The Hebrew word here is *hagah*, and the KJV translates it "meditate," but the picture it carries is physical.

*Hagah* is used in the Old Testament for a lion growling over its prey. It is used for a dove cooing. It is used in Isaiah for a man muttering. In every case the mouth is moving and the sound is low and repetitive.

That is not a quiet emptying of the mind. That is chewing.

**Meditation in the Word** — the sustained, spoken, repeated turning over of Scripture in the mind — is the practice being commanded. **Renewing of the Mind** — the replacing of your instincts with His — is the result. **The Word of God** — what He has spoken — is the thing being chewed.

Consider a man given a bitter tablet to dissolve under his tongue. He does not swallow it. He holds it there until it disperses. That is nearer to the biblical picture than any image of sitting blankly.

### Why It Is Attached to the Mouth

Notice the sentence construction: the book shall not depart out of thy mouth, and thou shalt meditate.

The mouth is mentioned first. Meditation in this verse is not silent reading. It is the spoken, half-spoken repetition of the text — saying it over, under your breath, until it has been said enough times to be believed.

That is why Scripture so often links the tongue to memory and the memory to the life. What you say repeatedly, you will eventually believe. What you believe, you will eventually do.

### Day and Night

The phrase "day and night" is not a demand for eighteen hours of study. It describes a rhythm — the text being the thing your mind returns to the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth.

The psalmist says it plainly: "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97). All the day. Not as a single appointment, but as a returning habit.

Set the two pictures side by side and the contrast is clear. One believer gives the Word a slot and then leaves it there, and finds that a week of pressure can outlast a year of reading. Another carries a verse through his day — into the traffic, into the meeting, into the washing up — and finds that the verse outlasts the pressure. Same text. Different exposure.

### The Result Is Not Information

The verse says meditation produces observation — "that thou mayest observe to do." The order matters.

Meditation does not produce a person who knows what to do. It produces a person who does it. The Word gets into the reflexes.

That is the whole point. In the moment of temptation, of fear, of sudden crisis, you do not have time to consult. You answer from what has already been digested.

### The Tree by the Water

Psalm 1 gives the same picture in plant form: the man whose delight is in the law of the Lord, who meditates on it day and night, is "like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither" (Psalm 1:3).

The tree is not straining. It is located. Have you been trying to produce fruit from a place you never settled? It is drinking what it is planted next to, slowly, without drama, and the fruit appears in its season.

That is a mercy, because it removes the pressure to produce. A tree does not decide to bear fruit this week. It stays where the water is, and the fruit is the outcome.

### What Meditation Is Not

It is not emptying the mind. That is the opposite practice, taught in other traditions, and it has more in common with sleep than with Joshua 1.

It is not repeating a phrase to reach a trance state. The Word is not a mantra; it is a message, and the point is understanding it rather than dissolving it.

And it is not the same as volume. Reading ten chapters in a hurry is a fine thing, and there is a place for it — but it produces familiarity, not reflex. Meditation is the slow, fewer-verses, longer-dwell lane.

### How to Do It Without Adding a Burden

The practice itself is simple enough to describe in four moves, and it does not require an hour.

Read the verse aloud. Say it again, slowly, emphasising a different word each time. Ask what it says about God and about you. Then speak it back to God as your own word before you close the book.

Do that with one verse for one week, and you will have done more than a month of hurried chapters.

## What This Means for You

**1. Choose fewer verses for longer.** Pick one verse for this week and stay with it. Depth is not achieved by coverage.

**2. Say it out loud.** Meditation in Scripture is a mouthy activity. Let your tongue get involved.

**3. Carry it into the day.** Put the verse where you will meet it, and return to it in the gaps.

**4. Watch the reflexes.** The test of meditation is not what you remember in the study but what comes out under pressure.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the Word is worth chewing and will change the chewer. Wisdom says that the practice needs to fit an actual life — a carer cannot hold a two-hour vigil, and a beginner should start with five minutes daily rather than plan a monastery — and that a mind full of unprocessed grief may need a counsellor as well as a quiet room.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Joshua 1:1–9**, and note what repetition God uses to steady the man.
- **Choose one verse**, write it out, and put it somewhere you will see it daily.
- **Speak it aloud three times a day** — morning, midday, and before sleep.
- **Read Psalm 1** and note the tree it describes.
