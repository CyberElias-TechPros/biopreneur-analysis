Second Samuel 9 opens with a question nobody expected from a king at the height of his power.

"And David said, Is there yet any that is left of the house of Saul, that I may shew him kindness for Jonathan's sake?" (2 Samuel 9:1).

David is not looking for an ally. He is looking for a remnant of the house that hunted him — and he wants to be kind to it. That is not how kings usually treat the families of their predecessors.

A servant named Ziba knew of one. A grandson of Saul, son of Jonathan, living in Lo-debar, "and he was lame on both his feet" (2 Samuel 9:3).

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **your seat at the King's table was settled by covenant, not by your feet — and a believer who knows his seat begins to walk differently from one who is still asking whether he is welcome.**

## The Name of the Place

The town was called Lo-debar, and the name means "no pasture" — a place of no pasture, no provision, no rank. It was where a man went when he had no claim left.

Mephibosheth's story had begun badly. At five years old, when news came that Saul and Jonathan had died, his nurse took him up and fled, and in the hurry he fell and was lamed for life (2 Samuel 4:4). He did not choose his injury, and he did not choose his address. Both were handed to him by a day he could not control.

Consider a man who has held a modest position in a company, lost it through a merger he had nothing to do with, and now answers the phone in his old employer's name to men who used to report to him. He is not bitter, exactly. He is simply living at an address that tells him what he lost.

That is Lo-debar. It is less a location than a conclusion.

### What David Did Not Ask

Read the account slowly and note the questions the king does not ask.

He does not ask whether Mephibosheth can walk. He does not ask whether he has recovered, trained, or proven himself. He does not require an interview, a statement, or a season of probation. He asks one thing: are you of the house of Jonathan?

**Sonship** — the standing of a child in a father's household — was the whole qualification. **Righteousness** — right standing granted rather than earned — is the same principle in different language. **The Consciousness of Righteousness** — an inward awareness that settles a person — is what happens when the answer to that question finally lands.

Set the two positions side by side and the contrast is stark. One man assesses himself by what his feet can do, and lives at the address his injury gave him. Another is assessed by whose son he is, and travels to the table whether or not the feet cooperate. The first audits himself for a welcome; the second has already been given one.

### The Sentence That Holds Both Halves

"And David said unto him, Fear not: for I will surely shew thee kindness for Jonathan thy father's sake, and will restore thee all the land of Saul thy father; and thou shalt eat bread at my table continually" (2 Samuel 9:7).

Three gifts in one breath. Fear addressed. Inheritance restored. Table granted. And notice whose sake it is all for — not Mephibosheth's. For Jonathan's sake.

That is the shape of the gospel, arriving a thousand years early. You are received for the sake of another, and the receiving is not a reward for your gait.

### His Own Eyes, Not the King's

The next verse is the one most believers live in. "And he bowed himself, and said, What is thy servant, that thou shouldest look upon such a dead dog as I am?" (2 Samuel 9:8).

A dead dog. That is a man's estimate of himself in the presence of a king's kindness.

It was not humility, exactly. Humility agrees with God; this was disagreement with God, spoken politely. David had just called him a son of the covenant and given him a seat; Mephibosheth called himself a dead dog in the same room.

**Fear had been addressed and he was still afraid.** That is the condition of many sincere believers. They have been told the seat is theirs, and they keep answering from Lo-debar.

### The Feet Still Lame, the Seat Still Set

One detail is worth its weight in gold: the text does not say Mephibosheth was healed. Verse 13 says he dwelt in Jerusalem, "for he did eat continually at the king's table; and was lame on both his feet."

Both things are true at once. Lame feet, and a set table.

So the question is not whether you feel capable. It is whether you will take the seat while the feet are still catching up. And here is where a believer's walk changes: a man who knows his seat stops performing for admission and starts moving from belonging. He speaks with the ease of someone already received.

### The Table Is Ongoing

The Hebrew word behind "continually" carries the sense of all the days, without interruption. This was not a welcome dinner. It was a standing arrangement.

That is why the New Testament keeps describing salvation in household language rather than courtroom language alone. You were not merely acquitted. You were brought in. Ephesians says it plainly: "Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19).

A household has addresses, meals, and names. It also has a Father who is not embarrassed by the family He has chosen.

## What This Means for You

**1. Answer from the covenant, not the injury.** What would you say about yourself if you started from whose you are rather than from what happened to you? Try saying it that way this week.

**2. Stop auditing your welcome.** Repeated self-examination for acceptance is not humility; it is unbelief wearing humility's clothes.

**3. Take the seat before the feet agree.** Come to the table while you still feel unimpressive. The seating is not contingent on your composure.

**4. Expect the walk to follow the seat, not the reverse.** Behaviour grows out of identity rather than producing it. Give the identity time to work outward.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says you are a child of the King and speaks accordingly. Wisdom says that being a King's child includes keeping His household's habits — honesty, diligence, restraint, and care for the people in the house with you. Faith settles the seat; wisdom decides the manners.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read 2 Samuel 9:1–13 in one sitting**, and mark every phrase David speaks about what he will do.
- **Write the sentence you usually say about yourself**, then write what the covenant says instead.
- **Before prayer this week, say aloud: "I am here as a son, not as a supplicant."**
- **Read Romans 8:14–17**, and note what the Spirit enables you to say.
