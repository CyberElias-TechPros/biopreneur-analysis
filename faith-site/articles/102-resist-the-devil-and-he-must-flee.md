There is a sentence in James that reads like a promise and is actually a procedure: "Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7).

Most believers know the second half. They have quoted it in the middle of a hard night, sometimes through gritted teeth, and wondered afterward why the pressure seemed unchanged. The problem is rarely the promise. It is that the first half was skipped, and the two halves are welded together by a single "therefore." Who exactly is being resisted by a man who has not first submitted?

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **resistance is not a mood of defiance but a settled refusal anchored in submission — and the promise attached to it is not that the devil leaves quietly, but that he leaves.**

## The Order James Insists On

Look at the grammar of the verse, because it is not a list of two independent commands. "Submit yourselves therefore to God" comes first, and the resisting flows out of it. A man cannot stand against an authority he is not himself under. That is not a limitation God imposed; it is the structure of authority itself.

The picture is a soldier under orders. He does not resist the enemy because he is personally brave or because he has decided the campaign needs him. He resists because his commander assigned him a line to hold, and he holds it under command. Strip away the command and his resistance becomes a private quarrel with a far stronger opponent.

**Spiritual Warfare** — the conflict over what you will believe and say — is never a contest of raw force. **The Word of God** — God's own address to humanity, living and active — is the weapon Scripture names for it. **The Authority of the Believer** — the right and ability to stand in His name — is what submission *produces*, not what it costs.

Notice what James says in the very next breath: "Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you" (James 4:8). The letter is not teaching a technique for winning fights. It is teaching a posture that makes the fight winnable.

### What Resistance Actually Is

The Greek word is *anthistemi* — to stand against, to set oneself in opposition. It is a military term, and it is the same word used in 1 Peter 5:9: "Whom resist stedfast in the faith."

Two things follow from the word itself.

Resistance is *positional*. It is something you take, not something you perform. You do not chase the enemy around the room; you refuse to give him the ground you are standing on. Most spiritual exhaustion comes from believers who are trying to be aggressive in a direction God never sent them.

Resistance is *sustained*. "Resist" is a present imperative — a continuous action, an ongoing stance rather than a single decisive moment. It looks less like a shout and more like a decision you keep making while nothing appears to be happening.

Consider a boundary wall. The wall does not attack the river. It simply refuses to be moved, and the water finds another route. That is resistance: not force applied against force, but a refusal that redirects.

### The Model in the Wilderness

The clearest example of resistance in Scripture is the one who did it perfectly. In Matthew 4, Jesus is led into the wilderness and tempted three times. Watch His method, because it is startlingly ordinary.

Each time, the devil proposes something, and each time Jesus answers with a quotation from Deuteronomy. Not a rebuke. Not a display of power. Not an argument about the devil's credentials. "It is written" — and the quotation is specific to the proposal.

The wilderness account also reveals the shape of the enemy's approach. The first temptation is appetite; the second is presumption dressed as faith; the third is a shortcut to the nations that were already promised. He does not usually come with obvious evil. He comes with something close to the truth, and asks you to take it out of God's timing.

Look at what happened afterward: "And when the devil had ended all the temptation, he departed from him for a season" (Luke 4:13). For a season. The fleeing of James 4:7 is real, and it is not the end of the war. That keeps us from two errors at once — the error of thinking resistance failed because the pressure returns, and the error of thinking a single victory ends the campaign.

Set them side by side and the gap shows. One believer treats resistance as a performance he must get right, and judges himself by how quickly the pressure lifts. The other treats it as a stance he keeps, and judges himself by whether he is still standing under God's hand. The first is exhausted by the second week. The second is still there in the twentieth.

## What This Means for You

**1. Start with submission, every single time.** Before you say a word against the enemy, settle who you are under. Submission is not grovelling; it is alignment. The centurion in Matthew 8 understood this precisely — he commanded soldiers because he was himself a man under authority (Matthew 8:9). The same logic holds here. A believer who has not submitted has no line to hold.

**2. Use the written Word, specifically.** Jesus did not respond to the wilderness temptations with general encouragement; He answered each proposal with a text that addressed it. When a specific accusation lands, find the specific scripture that answers it, and speak that. Vagueness is what makes resistance feel weak.

**3. Refuse to negotiate.** What would it look like to bargain with an accuser who has already been disarmed? Resistance has no clause in it that allows a conversation. James frames the alternative bluntly two verses later: "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" is followed by a call to cleanse hands and purify hearts, not to debate the terms of surrender. You are not being asked to reach an agreement; you are being asked to decline one.

**4. Expect the return, and do not read it as failure.** Luke says the devil departed "for a season." A returning temptation is not evidence that your resistance did not work; it is evidence that you are still in a war. The instruction is the same on the second visit as on the first.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom guard the ordinary doors.** James's own context is unflattering — he has just described wars and fights arising from desires (James 4:1–2). Much of what feels like demonic pressure has a door in the believer's own habits, and wisdom closes doors. Faith resists the enemy; wisdom stops leaving the gate open. Both are obedience, and neither substitutes for the other.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read James 4:1–10 as one unit** and write down what James says comes before and after the resistance.
- **Identify one specific pressure** you are facing and find one specific scripture that answers it, writing both on the same page.
- **Practice one act of submission daily** this week — a deliberate, spoken yielding of a decision you would rather control.
- **Read Matthew 4:1–11 and Luke 4:13 together** and notice both the method and the "for a season."
