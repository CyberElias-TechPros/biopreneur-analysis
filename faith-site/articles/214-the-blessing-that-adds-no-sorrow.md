Everyone who has watched money arrive knows that a great deal of it comes with a bill nobody itemised.

The promotion that costs the marriage. The deal that costs the conscience. The windfall that costs the boy who was never taught what to do with money, and now has too much of it.

**Proverbs 10:22** draws a line across the middle of that experience: "The blessing of the LORD, it maketh rich, and he addeth no sorrow with it."

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **God's blessing is distinguishable from ordinary gain by what it does not bring with it, and the difference is not in the amount but in the hand it came from.**

## Reading the Verse Carefully

### It Says "Maketh Rich"

Start with what the verse actually promises, because some teachers have been embarrassed by it.

*The blessing of the LORD, it maketh rich.* Not "the blessing of the Lord makes content." Not "makes sufficient." The Hebrew is *ʿāshar* — to become wealthy, to gain riches.

Scripture is not squeamish about abundance. Abraham was "very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold" (Genesis 13:2). Isaac sowed and reaped a hundredfold in a famine year (Genesis 26:12). **The Bible does not apologise for blessing.**

### It Names the Source

The middle of the sentence is the load-bearing part: *the blessing of the LORD.*

There is a great deal of wealth in the world that has nothing to do with the blessing of God. It came from industry, luck, inherited advantage, or injustice. The verse is not a claim that all riches are blessings; it is a claim that God's kind of wealth comes from God's kind of source.

**The question is not how much you have. It is whose hand it came out of.**

### It Promises No Sorrow

The Hebrew for sorrow there is *ʿeṣeb* — the same word used of the pain of labour in Genesis 3:16, and of the toil of hard work in Genesis 3:17.

So the promise is that this blessing comes without the ache. Not without effort — diligence is commanded all through Proverbs — but without the **wound** that so often accompanies gain.

**God's blessing does not leave you nursing an injury you cannot name.**

### The Contrast Is Real

Proverbs 15 sets the two kinds of prosperity side by side: "Better is little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure and trouble therewith" (Proverbs 15:16).

Then a second time, in a different register: "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith" (Proverbs 15:17).

**Both verses are saying the same thing: the amount is not the measure. The room is.**

### Where Sorrow Comes From

#### Comparison

Most of the sorrow attached to money is not debt; it is comparison.

James explains the mechanism with uncomfortable precision: "Your gold and silver is cankered; and the rust of them shall be a witness against you... Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth" (James 5:3–4).

He is describing wealth accrued through injustice — and the warning is that such gain becomes evidence against its owner. Not every rich man has a clean ledger, and the ledger always surfaces.

#### Refusing the Blame

1 Timothy gives the verse that is very often misquoted: "For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows" (1 Timothy 6:10).

Note the phrase *pierced themselves through with many sorrows.* The sorrow is not a punishment imposed from outside. It is the natural outcome of a wound that the person inflicted on themselves by the thing they would not let go of.

**Money is not the problem. The grip is.**

#### The Cost of the Omission

Solomon's warning in Proverbs 30 turns on a single word: "lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD?" (Proverbs 30:9).

The sorrow that gain brings is not always financial. Sometimes it is spiritual — a subtle, comfortable forgetting. **Prosperity can quietly remove the need you once felt for God, and that is the costliest sorrow of all.**

#### The Alternative: No Sorrow, No Shame

The verse opposite Proverbs 10:22 is Psalm 127:2: "It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep."

*Bread of sorrows* — the same Hebrew again. The psalm is not promising that the diligent will be poor. It is promising that they will not have to live on the wages of their own anxiety.

**God's version of abundance includes sleep, and anxiety's version never does.**

#### The Boundary

One honest limit: this proverb is a general truth about God's ways, not a guarantee that every believer will be wealthy or that every sorrowful rich person deserves what happened to them.

Job was rich and righteous and lost everything; his friends built a whole theology on the opposite assumption and were rebuked by God (Job 42:7). The verse describes the character of God's blessing. It does not run everyone else's life.

## What This Means for You

**1. Ask about the hand, not just the sum.** Before you pursue an opportunity, ask where it comes from and what it will cost.

**2. Audit the sorrow you already carry.** Is something you own quietly charging you a fee in worry, or in your family, or in your conscience?

**3. Refuse comparison.** Comparison turns a blessed amount into a disappointing one without changing the amount.

**4. Practise thanksgiving at the current level.** Gratitude is the discipline that keeps gain clean, because it keeps the giver in view.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** Believe for the blessing and mind the ledger. **Faith and Wisdom** are friends; a clean business practice is not a lack of faith.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Proverbs 10:22 every morning** this week.
- **Read 1 Timothy 6:6–10** and mark the word "sorrows."
- **Write down one gain** and honestly ask what came with it.
- **Read Psalm 127:1–2** at bedtime.
- **Give something away** this week, deliberately, to keep your hand loose.
