Have you ever caught yourself apologising for a habit as though it still owned you — "I cannot help it, that is just how I am"? It sounds honest. But Romans 6:6 announces a legal fact that changes the sentence entirely: your old man is not dying slowly under your effort; he **is crucified** with Christ. Past tense. Finished.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the old man — your Adamic, sin-ruled identity before Christ — was crucified with Him at the cross, so you are no longer obligated to serve his appetites.** You do not need another funeral for a man who already died. You need to stop digging.

## What "Old Man" Actually Means

Paul's phrase in Romans 6:6 is *ho palaios anthropos* — literally, the old, worn-out human. *Palaios* does not mean merely elderly; it means belonging to an expired order, obsolete, no longer in force. The old man is not your body, and he is not your personality. He is the identity you carried in Adam: separated from God, ruled by sin's appetites, and convinced that your worth and future were decided by yesterday.

That is why Paul can say in the same breath that the old man was crucified *with him* — with Christ. **The Finished Work** — the complete, once-for-all redemptive accomplishment of Christ's death and resurrection — did not leave the old man on life support. It brought his legal reign to an end. Colossians 3:3 puts it in the same settled tense: "For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God." Dead — not recovering. Hid — not on display for sin to command.

Think of a house that has been sold and the new owner has moved in. The old tenant's name still sits on a mailbox down the road, and old mail still arrives. But the tenant has no legal right to enter, no keys to the door, and no authority over the new household. If the new owner keeps paying the old tenant's bills out of habit, the problem is not that the lease is still valid. The problem is that the new owner has not yet learned to refuse the old address. That is the picture behind this teaching: the old man's appetites still knock, but his ownership has ended.

### Crucified Is a Finished Legal Fact, Not a Daily Struggle

Notice what Paul says the crucifixion accomplished: "that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin." The purpose clause is decisive. The old man was crucified so that you would **not serve** sin anymore. Serving implies a master. Paul is announcing that your master changed.

Why, then, do old appetites still feel so loud? Because the body and the mind still carry memories of the old regime. **Renewing of the Mind** — replacing old, fearful, flesh-shaped habits of thought with God's way of thinking through steady meditation and practice of Scripture — is the daily work of bringing your thinking into agreement with a verdict already given. You are not renewing your spirit; your spirit was made new at the New Birth. You are renewing your mind so that your choices stop voting for a dead man.

Here the two diverge. Religion tells you to bury the old man a little more every day, as though your effort keeps him dead. The gospel tells you he died once with Christ, and your part is to **reckon** it so — to count it true and act accordingly. Burial is effort. Reckoning is agreement with fact.

### The Word Says the Same Thing From Three Directions

- **Romans 6:11** — "Likewise reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord." Paul does not say, "Try to become dead to sin." He says reckon yourselves dead. Reckoning is accounting language: count what heaven counts, even before your feelings catch up.
- **Galatians 5:24** — "And they that are Christ's have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts." The crucifixion of the flesh is presented as the settled position of those who belong to Christ — not as a future achievement for the most disciplined believers.
- **2 Corinthians 5:14** — "For the love of Christ constraineth us; because we thus judge, that if one died for all, then were all dead." Paul's logic is corporate and legal: because Christ died for all, all died with Him. The old man's death is not individual heroism; it is union with Christ.

Three letters, one verdict: the old man died with Christ, and the believer's freedom begins by agreeing with that death rather than re-enacting it.

## What This Means for You

**1. You are dead to sin's ownership, alive to God's.** Romans 6:11 joins two reckonings that must never be separated. Dead to sin means sin no longer owns you. Alive unto God means God now owns your obedience. Freedom is never neutral; it is a transfer of allegiance.

**2. Old appetites are memories, not masters.** When an old desire knocks — anger, dishonesty, lust, fear, bitterness — you are not facing your true nature. You are facing a memory of an expired regime. Memories can be loud, but they cannot legally command a new creation.

**3. Stop burying; start reckoning.** Every time you treat the old man as still alive — rehearsing his excuses, negotiating with his appetites, or confessing yourself as his prisoner — you are digging a grave for a man heaven already buried. Reckoning is stronger than remorse: "That is not who I am in Christ."

**4. Faith and wisdom walk together here.** Faith declares the finished verdict; wisdom builds new habits that agree with it. Faith and practical wisdom work together as you set boundaries, seek accountability, rest well, and choose environments that support holiness. Declaring freedom never replaces diligent, responsible action — it empowers it.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Name the old man's knock out loud.** When a familiar appetite speaks this week, answer it by name: "You belong to the old man who died with Christ. I reckon myself dead to you and alive unto God." Spoken reckoning trains the mind faster than silent shame.
- **Read Romans 6 aloud each morning.** Do not skim it as history. Read it as your current account: crucified, destroyed, not serving, reckoning, alive. What you hear repeatedly becomes what you count on.
- **Replace one burial ritual with one reckoning practice.** If you have been apologising to yourself for an old habit, replace the apology with a confession to your Father (1 John 1:9) plus a concrete new habit that expresses the new man.
- **Audit your week by ownership, not by mood.** At week's end, ask: "Where did I act as though the old man still owned me?" Then thank your Father for grace to walk in step with the new life He created in you.
