The sentence comes at the end of the parable of the sower, and it is addressed to people who had just heard the same sermon as everybody else.

"Take heed therefore how ye hear: for whosoever hath, to him shall be given; and whosoever hath not, from him shall be taken even that which he seemeth to have" (Luke 8:18).

He does not say take heed *what* you hear — that is important too, but it is not this verse. He says take heed *how*.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the Word does not enter everyone the same way — the manner of hearing is a decision, and the person who listens carelessly loses even the little he thought he had.**

## The Command Behind "Take Heed"

The Greek is *blepete* — keep looking, watch, be on your guard. It is a Present imperative, which means continuous attention rather than a single moment of care.

And the object is not the content. It is the hearing itself.

**The Word of God** — what He has spoken — is the seed falling on all soil alike. **Renewing of the Mind** — the reshaping of your thinking — is what a good ear produces. **The Life of Faith** — acting on what is heard — is the crop.

Consider two men at the same service, one row apart. One listens to argue — he is scanning for the point he disagrees with. Another listens to obey — he is scanning for the thing he must do. By Monday, one has a memory of a service, the other has a change. Neither the preacher nor the text was different.

### Four Soils, One Seed

Luke 8 tells us the soils in advance, and every one of them is a description of hearing rather than of the Word.

The wayside hears and loses it to the enemy immediately. The rock hears and receives joyfully, but has no root, and falls when testing comes. The thorns hear and are choked by "cares and riches and pleasures of this life" (Luke 8:14). The good ground hears "with an honest and good heart" and holds it, and brings forth fruit with patience (Luke 8:15).

Notice that the seed is never the variable. The same Word, the same authority, the same speaker. Everything that differs is in the ear and the soil.

That is a sobering arrangement, because it puts the outcome partly in our hands. Which soil have you been lately? It also means nobody can say the message was inadequate.

### The Lamp and the Bed

Just before this verse, Jesus gives the small parable of the lamp: "No man, when he hath lighted a lamp, covereth it with a vessel, or putteth it under a bed; but setteth it on a candlestick, that they which come in may see the light" (Luke 8:16).

The connection is not accidental. Received light is meant to be visible. A person who hears well begins to shine — not because he is trying to be noticed, but because light that is looked after does that.

The one who hears carelessly is, in this image, putting the lamp under furniture. It is still a lamp. It is simply not doing anything. What have you been covering yours with?

### The Strange Economics of Verse 18

Then comes the sentence that sounds almost unfair: to him who has shall be given; from him who has not shall be taken even what he seems to have.

That is not arbitrary. It is describing a law of increase and decay that runs through the whole of life.

A skill practised grows. A skill neglected quietly disappears. A language half-learned as a child leaves a man certain he knows it and unable to speak it. What he "seemeth to have" is exactly the phrase — the appearance outliving the substance.

Which means the truth you hear and do not act on is in the worst possible position. It is not neutral. You now have the illusion of possession without the possession, and that illusion makes you less likely to seek the thing itself.

### Why "How" Comes Before "What"

This is the practical edge of the verse, and it is worth stating bluntly.

You can attend a great deal of good teaching with a distracted ear and be worse off than before, because the familiarity dulls appetite. That is what the writer of Hebrews was describing when he said they were "dull of hearing" and needed milk rather than meat (Hebrews 5:11–14).

Set the two listeners side by side once more. One goes to everything, sits at the back, checks his phone, feels vaguely encouraged, and changes nothing. Another goes less often, takes one truth, writes it down, and does something with it before the week ends. The first is well-fed and malnourished. The second is growing.

### The Honest Heart

The good-soil description is worth memorising, because it tells you what a good ear actually is: "an honest and good heart" that "having heard the word, keep it, and bring forth fruit with patience."

Three things. Honesty — no hidden agenda in the listening. Keeping — not letting it slip away by Monday. Patience — accepting that fruit takes a season.

And "keep it" is a holding word, the kind you use of something valuable you put somewhere safe until it is needed.

## What This Means for You

**1. Decide before you sit down.** Say to yourself, on the way in, what you are listening for. It changes what you catch.

**2. Bring something to write with.** Whatever the ear catches is being carried out of the room on something.

**3. Keep one thing per service.** Deliberately choose one truth to carry out. Keeping nine is a way of keeping none.

**4. Ask the "what now" question.** Every truth has an action or it is only an impression.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says listening is worth effort and produces fruit. Wisdom says that means choosing your teachers carefully as well — a good ear under bad teaching accumulates error faster — and that "honest and good heart" includes asking mature believers whether you have heard rightly.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Luke 8:4–18** in one sitting, and mark every phrase about how people heard.
- **Choose one sermon or chapter this week** and take a single truth from it.
- **Write that truth on a card** and keep it until you have acted on it.
- **Read James 1:19–21**, and notice the receiving posture James names.
