Few things in the Christian life have been taught as badly as this, and the proof is how many sincere believers have flushed medication down a sink in the name of faith.

"Drink no longer water, but use a little wine for thy stomach's sake and thine often infirmities" (1 Timothy 5:23).

That is an apostle, writing Scripture, prescribing a remedy to a sick minister.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **faith and medicine are not rival powers competing for your trust; they are the same Father working through a promise and a means, and the man who refuses the means has not added to his faith.**

## What the Verse Establishes

### The Command, Not the Suggestion

Start with the grammar, because it is not a suggestion. "Drink no longer water" is an imperative, and it is followed by another: "use a little wine."

Paul is instructing Timothy, a man he had personally discipled, to stop his practice of drinking only water — presumably a scrupulous abstention — for the sake of his stomach and his frequent illnesses.

So the New Testament contains a direct apostolic instruction to a sick believer to take a remedy. That single verse settles a great deal of argument.

### Why This Verse Cannot Be Explained Away

Some readers have tried to soften it. "Wine was safer than water" — plausible, and Paul's concern for Timothy's stomach is compatible with it. But note that he says "for thine often infirmities," which is broader than water quality.

Others have said it was only for Timothy, not a principle. But the reason Paul gives is ordinary and physical: a stomach, and recurring illness.

And notice the timing. Paul does not say "pray about it and get back to me." He gives a prescription. If faith alone were the required response to Timothy's stomach, Paul would have been undermining Timothy's faith by answering as he did.

### Luke, the Physician

And then there is the quiet testimony of the New Testament's own personnel.

Paul, writing from a Roman prison, sends this greeting: "Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you" (Colossians 4:14).

*Iatros*, physician — the standard Greek word for a doctor. Luke is described with the ordinary word for his ordinary profession, and he is "beloved."

Now consider what that means. The same Luke who wrote the gospel and Acts accompanied Paul on journeys and stayed with him in his final imprisonment. If physicians were an alternative to the Spirit, Paul would not have kept one beside him in the dark.

And Luke's own writings give the same picture. The Good Samaritan did not pray over the wounded man by the road; he poured in oil and wine and bound up his wounds and put him on his own beast (Luke 10:34). **Jesus told that story as the definition of a neighbour.**

### Hezekiah and the Fig

There is an older pattern, and it holds both halves in one account.

Hezekiah was told by the prophet, "Set thine house in order; for thou shalt die, and not live" (2 Kings 20:1). He turned his face to the wall and prayed, and wept sore. And God answered and promised him fifteen more years.

And then what did Isaiah say? "Take a lump of figs. And they took and laid it on the boil, and he recovered" (2 Kings 20:7).

**The promise came, and then a poultice came with it.** The healing was God's; the means was a lump of figs. Neither cancelled the other.

### Why the Confusion Exists

So why does the church get this wrong so often? Three reasons.

**A misunderstanding of what faith is.** Faith is not a state of being sufficiently confident about something. It is trust in a Person. Trusting God does not require distrusting your pharmacist.

**A fear of looking faithless.** Many believers quietly believe that taking medicine proves they never really believed. So they refuse it, and call the fear faith.

**A few bad experiences.** Someone heard a sermon that turned them into a sceptic about testimony, or someone they loved died while refusing treatment because a preacher told them to. Both are real wounds, and both deserve better than a slogan.

### What Faith Actually Does

So the positive account. What does faith do in relation to medicine?

Faith **names the source**. When medicine works, faith knows who is behind it. "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above" (James 1:17) — including the medication, including the prescriber, including the training.

Faith **refuses to let the means displace the Fountain**. The gift is not the source. Taking a tablet is not trusting a tablet.

Faith **prays while it takes**. That is the pattern of James: "the prayer of faith shall save the sick" (James 5:15), and the context has the elders anointing with oil. Oil in the ancient world was, among other things, a medicinal substance. **The prayer came with a means and the means with a prayer.**

### The Doctrine and the Word Picture

One illustration, and it is drawn from what a farmer actually does.

A farmer cannot grow a single seed. He has no power over germination; nobody in history has ever persuaded a field to produce by sheer will. But he does not say that. He ploughs. He plants. He waters. He irrigates.

And he is not accused of unbelief for any of it.

The farmer's labour is not a substitute for God's gift; it is the practice of a man who trusts that the gift will come through the ordinary means of soil.

That is the Christian's relation to medicine precisely. Prayer is the plough; medicine is the watering; God gives the growth; and nobody in the field thinks the two are arguing.

### The Other Error

But the article would be dishonest if it only corrected one direction. So the other word.

There is a way of taking the doctor's help that leaves God entirely out of it. It is not in this article, but it appears in real lives every day: the man who prays nothing, expects nothing, and takes everything, and thinks he has been reasonable.

**Faith and Wisdom is a pair for a reason.** Wisdom without faith has expectations no harder to defend than faith without wisdom. If you can pray for the healing, pray for it. If you can ask the church to anoint you, ask.

### When the Refusal Is Right

One nuance, because a careful reader will ask it.

Scripture does record times when the right thing was refusal. Daniel refused meat offered to idols; the Hebrew children refused to bow; Peter and John refused the council's command. But every one of those refusals was about worship and loyalty, not about medicine.

And there is a related case. A treatment may be refused when it is a matter of conscience — a blood product for some believers, or a treatment the person believes violates the Word. That is between them and God, and this article makes no verdict on another's servant.

But "I am afraid that taking this would prove my faith was weak" is not conscience. It is fear, and perfect love has something better for it.

## What This Means for You

**1. Take the medicine.** If you are unwell and you have been treating the prescription as a faithlessness, Paul has a verb for you: use it.

**2. Keep praying while you do.** The goal is not to transfer trust to the means but to bring the means under the greater trust. **The Life of Faith** is a whole-life posture, not a single decision about a bottle.

**3. Ask whether you would be comfortable saying "God healed me"** if a doctor had helped. If your testimony requires the absence of a doctor, your doctrine has shrunk.

**4. Encourage the believer who is struggling.** Nobody watching *Divine Wholeness* as a standard is strengthened by being told their medication is their doubt.

**5. Get the help you have been avoiding.** Not merely medical — the counselling, the conversation, the rest. Faith is not a reason to stay in a hole; it is strength to climb out with help.

- **Take one avoided step**: the appointment, the prescription, the check-up.
- **Say it plainly**: "Thank You for the medicine."
