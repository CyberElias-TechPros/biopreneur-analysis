Paul says something in 1 Corinthians 15 that sounds like a call to effort and is in fact a call to arithmetic.

"Awake to righteousness, and sin not; for some have not the knowledge of God: I speak this to your shame" (1 Corinthians 15:34).

Read it the wrong way and it becomes another demand: *try harder, be better*. Read it the way Paul wrote it — in a chapter about resurrection, to a church arguing that there is no resurrection — and the sense changes entirely.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **righteousness is not a standard you are climbing toward; it is a standing you have been given and have not yet woken up to, and accusation is what keeps a person asleep in a room they already own.**

## What "Awake" Means Here

### The Greek Verb Is About Alertness

The word is *eknēphō* — to sober up, to come out of a stupor, to be roused from drunkenness.

Paul is not telling the Corinthians to manufacture righteousness. He is telling them to come out of a state in which they have forgotten what is true.

**You cannot become what you already are; you can only fail to notice it.**

### Why the Resurrection Matters to This

The chapter is about the resurrection of the body, and Paul closes it with an ethical appeal: because the dead are raised, "be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58).

Righteousness and resurrection are linked because both are things God did while we were passive.

**The same posture that receives a resurrection receives a verdict: neither is earned, and both must be woken up to.**

### The Sleep of Accusation

What keeps a believer asleep in a righteousness they already possess? Paul names it elsewhere — a mind that has not been renewed, still running the old court case.

An accused person does not build. They defend. And a believer who lives in the dock will produce exactly the fruit of someone still on trial.

**Accusation is not merely unpleasant; it is atmosphere, and it determines what grows.**

### The Courtroom Paul Describes

#### The Charges and the Answer

Romans 8 asks the questions in legal form: "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again" (Romans 8:33–34).

Follow the logic. The one who would have to bring the charge is the one who has already justified. The judge is the defence.

**The case cannot be reopened because the presiding authority closed it Himself.**

#### Zechariah's Picture

Zechariah 3 gives the same scene in vision: Joshua the high priest standing before the angel, with Satan at his right hand to resist him — and the LORD rebuking the accuser before anything is said about Joshua's fitness (Zechariah 3:1–2).

Then the command: "Take away the filthy garments from him" (Zechariah 3:4).

**The rebuke of the accuser comes before the change of clothes, because the verdict is not based on the clothes.**

#### Why the Accuser Has Nothing to Work With

He is called "the accuser of our brethren" (Revelation 12:10), and his entire operation is prosecution.

But a prosecutor needs a charge, and the charge has been answered in the body of Christ. There is a reason the gospel is described as a finished work rather than an ongoing trial.

**He is not the judge. He is a plaintiff with an empty file.**

### What a Waking Life Looks Like

#### It Begins With Agreement, Not Effort

The first move is not to try harder. It is to agree with God's verdict out loud — which is what confession means in the New Testament sense: *homologia*, saying the same thing.

"I believe therefore have I spoken" (2 Corinthians 4:13) is the pattern. Faith speaks before it feels.

**You do not wait to feel righteous before you speak like one.**

**Justification** is the legal fact; the **The Consciousness of Righteousness** is the settled awareness of it that changes how a person walks into a room.

#### It Shows Up as Boldness

"Herein is our love made perfect, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment: because as he is, so are we in this world" (1 John 4:17).

That is a remarkable sentence. The basis for boldness on judgment day is not a record but a resemblance — *as he is, so are we*.

**A person who is not afraid of the verdict behaves differently in every other room.**

#### It Reframes Failure

When a believer sins, the accusation says: *this is who you really are.* The gospel says: *this is not who you are, and it is beneath you.*

The short path back is confession and continued walking, not a probationary period of self-punishment (1 John 2:1–2).

Penance is a way of trying to pay a bill that has already been settled in full.

### The Boundary

Two guardrails, because this is the doctrine most easily abused.

First, waking to righteousness is not permission to sin. Paul's sentence explicitly says "and sin not." The point is that a settled identity produces restraint; it does not remove the obligation.

Second, this is not positive thinking. You are not declaring something into existence. You are agreeing with a verdict that God rendered at Calvary on the basis of Christ's work — an external, historical fact, not an internal mood.

**The **Righteousness** you are waking to is not a feeling you generate. It is a fact you report.**

## What This Means for You

**1. Notice the accusation.** Learn to recognise the voice that speaks in verdicts. It rarely offers evidence; it offers atmosphere.

**2. Answer with the verdict, not with your record.** When accused, quote Romans 8:33 rather than listing your better days.

**3. Speak it before you feel it.** Say the thing aloud in the morning.

**4. Stop doing penance.** If you have failed, confess, and continue. Do not build a season of self-punishment.

**5. Keep faith and wisdom together.** Waking up is a discipline, not a single revelation — it is renewed by the Word, by fellowship, and by practice. **Faith and Wisdom** returns to the verdict every day, the way a man returns to a mirror.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Romans 8:31–39** every morning this week.
- **Write down the three accusations** you hear most often.
- **Answer each one** with a verse.
- **Read Zechariah 3:1–5** and notice the order of events.
- **Say your declaration aloud** before you open your phone.
