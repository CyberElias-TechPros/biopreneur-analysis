There is a courtroom scene in Zechariah that gives language to something every believer has felt. "And he shewed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to resist him" (Zechariah 3:1).

The high priest is not accused of a private failing. He is standing in the place of a whole nation, and he is wearing the wrong clothes — "clothed with filthy garments" (Zechariah 3:3). The accuser has a case. And the first words of the scene are not an argument, a defence, or an admission. They are a rebuke from the Lord: "The LORD rebuke thee, O Satan" (Zechariah 3:2), followed immediately by new clothing.

Hold that scene in your mind as you come to Revelation: "And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony; and they loved not their lives unto the death" (Revelation 12:11). Here is the central truth of this teaching: **a testimony is not a memory you report but a verdict you repeat — and the accuser is silenced by what you say about Christ, not by what you say about yourself.**

## Who He Is and What He Does

The title matters. He is "the accuser of our brethren... which accused them before our God day and night" (Revelation 12:10). Twice in that verse the work is named: accusing, and doing it relentlessly. This is not a being who tempts you to sin in the first place; that is a different activity. This is a being who then prosecutes you for it.

That distinction explains a great deal of the misery of sincere believers. Temptation comes and is resisted, and then comes the second wave — not another suggestion but a charge. "You call yourself a Christian." "You knew better." "Everyone in your family knows what you are like." The voice is not asking you to do anything. It is trying to install a verdict.

**The Blood of Jesus** — Christ's sacrificial life laid down on the cross, cleansing the conscience and securing remission — is the answer already filed. **Confession of the Word** (*homologia*, speaking the same thing God says) is the mouth that publishes it. **The Authority of the Believer** — the right to stand in His name — is what makes the publishing an act of legal standing rather than wishful thinking.

Think of a court clerk who reads a verdict aloud. The clerk has no power to alter the judgment; the judge decided it. But the reading is not decoration. Until it is read, the room is at the mercy of whoever is shouting loudest.

### Why the Blood Comes First

Notice the order and do not rush it: the blood *of the Lamb*, and the word of their *testimony*. The second rests on the first. A testimony without the blood is a person describing themselves; a testimony that arises from the blood is a person describing what has been done for them.

This is where many well-meaning believers get into trouble. They attempt to answer accusation with self-improvement narratives. "I'm getting better." "I'm not as bad as I used to be." These are not testimonies in the Revelation 12:11 sense; they are defences, and a defence is exactly the move the accuser is waiting for. He is not hoping you will be silent. He is hoping you will argue on the merits.

The blood does not argue on the merits. It settles them.

### What a Testimony Actually Says

The word in Revelation is *martyria* — witness, evidence, the testimony of a witness in court. It is the root of the word our Bibles use for martyr, and the last clause of the verse explains why: "and they loved not their lives unto the death."

A witness is someone who tells what he has seen, under oath, without regard to what it costs him. That is a different act from giving your opinion. And notice what the testimony in Revelation 12:11 is a testimony *of*. It is not a testimony about the believer's progress. It is the "testimony of Jesus" — the church is described elsewhere by exactly that phrase (Revelation 12:17). What you are saying in the courtroom is not "look how far I have come." It is "look at what He did."

Set them side by side and the gap shows. The accused man builds a case from his own record, and his record will always have holes in it. The witness states what the blood has accomplished, and the blood has no holes. One testimony is strengthened by a good week. The other is unchanged by a bad one.

### The Testimony in Ordinary Rooms

This is where the teaching usually turns abstract, and it should not. The Greek word for testimony also carried a very ordinary sense in the ancient world: the evidence a person gives in a legal matter, and the reputation of a witness. Both senses belong together.

Your testimony is spoken in two places. In your prayer life, where you answer accusation out loud with what the Word says — that is the courtroom. And in front of people, where you say what God has done — that is the publishing.

The second is riskier and more costly than we usually admit. Revelation adds the clause about not loving their lives unto death precisely because the testimony in view was being given by people under threat. Speaking the truth about Christ in a room that has decided against Him can cost employment, family peace, a friend's respect. The verse says the way through that is a testimony that has already settled who owns the life being threatened.

### The Accuser's Forfeited Court

It is worth remembering what happened in these two scenes, because the position of the accuser is not what it once was. In Zechariah the rebuke comes from the Lord. In Revelation the accuser is described as having been cast down, and the overcoming in verse 11 is the reason given for the rejoicing in verse 12.

And the New Testament makes the legal situation explicit: "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth?" (Romans 8:33–34). Paul's answer to the prosecutor is not a defence of the defendant. It is the identity of the Judge.

The accuser still speaks. A dismissed prosecutor sometimes stands outside the courthouse shouting at people who have already been acquitted — and the acquitted can walk past because the case is closed, not because the shouting has stopped.

## What This Means for You

**1. Answer accusation with the blood, not with your record.** When the charge comes — and it will, usually at night and usually in the second person — do not reach for your best recent behaviour. Reach for the cross. "The blood of Jesus answers that" is not a magic sentence; it is a true one, and it moves the discussion from your performance to His payment.

**2. Speak your testimony out loud, even alone.** There is something about audible speech that keeps the mind honest. Write out three sentences: what Christ did, what it secured, and what that makes true of you. Say them when the pressure comes. That is homologia in action.

**3. Do not confuse honesty with surrender.** Owning a fault to your Father and receiving cleansing (1 John 1:9) is not the same as agreeing with a verdict of condemnation. The first is confession, and it ends in forgiveness. The second is an agreement with the enemy, and it ends in hiding. Know which conversation you are in.

**4. Testify about Christ in front of someone this week.** The testimony was never designed to live entirely inside your prayer life. Risk a sentence about what He has done in front of a person who does not have the same hope. Nothing cures private despair faster than speaking publicly about a real answer.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom use the right occasion.** Faith says your testimony is true; wisdom reads the room. Not every setting is a courtroom ready to hear, and a believer who cannot tell the difference is not braver, only careless. Tell the truth quietly where it will be shouted down, and tell it fully where it will be heard.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Zechariah 3:1–5 slowly** and note what changes the priest's clothing.
- **Write three sentences of testimony** — the blood, the result, your position — and say them aloud each morning.
- **Answer one recurring charge in writing** with Romans 8:33–34.
- **Tell one person** what Christ has done, this week, in your own words.
