Jesus says this in the middle of a conversation about the end of the world.

His disciples have just pointed out the temple buildings, and He has told them not one stone will be left on another. They ask when. He answers with wars, famines, earthquakes, persecution, and then a sentence that stops the whole sequence.

"Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away" (Matthew 24:35).

He puts His words in a category more durable than the sky. Here is the central truth of this teaching: **the Word of God does not expire with the age it was spoken into, which means a promise made four thousand years ago is as live today as the day it left His mouth.**

## Two Things Compared

The comparison is deliberately extreme. Not "my words will outlast this generation" or "my words will survive the temple." Heaven and earth.

Everything you can see — the hills behind your house, the stars you cannot count, the ground you will be buried in — is placed on one side of a scale and marked temporary. On the other side is a sentence.

**The Word of God** — what He has spoken — belongs to the permanent order of things. **Revelation Knowledge** — truth made known by Him rather than reasoned out — is how a person comes to know it. **The Life of Faith** — a life built on what He said — is what permanence makes possible.

Consider a man who builds a house on a riverbank because the view is lovely, and a man who builds on rock above the flood line. Both houses are sturdy. Only one is on ground that will still be there.

### Why "Pass Away" Is the Right Word

The Greek is *pareleusontai*, from a verb meaning to pass by, to go past, to slip away. It is the vocabulary of something moving out of view rather than something being destroyed.

That is precisely how the world behaves. It does not announce its ending; it simply keeps moving, and things you were sure of last year have quietly gone past. Currency, careers, health, reputations, institutions — all of them are in motion.

Into that motion Jesus puts a fixed point. Whatever else moves, this does not.

### The Old Testament Agrees

This is not a new idea introduced by Jesus. Isaiah had already said it: "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever" (Isaiah 40:8). And the psalmist: "For ever, O LORD, thy word is settled in heaven" (Psalm 119:89).

Peter takes the Isaiah line and applies it directly to the gospel he is preaching: "But the word of the Lord endureth for ever. And this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you" (1 Peter 1:25).

Notice what Peter does there. He takes a statement about permanence and points it at the message his readers had already heard. The eternal Word is not an abstraction on a shelf. It had arrived at their address.

### Permanence Is Not the Same as Relevance

Here is where a careful reader has to do some work, because there is a lazy way to use this doctrine and a true way.

The lazy way says: the Word is permanent, therefore everything in it applies to me exactly as written, in every detail, with no attention to whom it was said or why. That is how people end up building doctrinal houses on promises made to Joshua about real estate.

The true way says: the Word is permanent, therefore nothing in it has become untrue — and the work of the reader is to ask what kind of thing is being said. A promise to a specific person, a command for a specific covenant, and a statement about the character of God are all permanent in different ways.

Set them side by side and the difference is plain. One believer treats every verse as a personal guarantee and gets shipwrecked when one does not land. Another treats every verse as permanently true and works out what kind of truth it is, and he is steadied by the same Book.

### What the Permanence Is For

If the only point were that the Bible is old and reliable, this would be an article about archives.

But look at where Jesus places the statement. He says it in the middle of a passage about the future — about the very years His disciples are worried about. The permanence of His words is given as the ground of confidence for days they have not seen.

That is the practical use of this doctrine. You have decades ahead of you that you cannot see, and the only thing you can carry into them that will not change is what He has said.

### The Years You Have Not Lived

There is a reason this lands differently as life goes on.

A young believer often trusts the Word for the thing in front of him — an exam, a job, a healing, a decision. A believer further along begins to notice that the same verses have carried him through several different versions of his life. The verse did not have to be replaced when the situation changed.

That is permanence observed rather than argued. It is also why older saints tend to sound unhurried. They have watched the ground move and the sentence stay.

## What This Means for You

**1. Stop dating your promises.** Which verse have you quietly filed under "for another season"? Take it back out.

**2. Build on the fixed point deliberately.** When you make a decision this week, name the Scripture you are standing on. A decision without a text is a preference.

**3. Let the Word outlast your circumstance.** The situation in front of you is temporary by definition. Treat it that way.

**4. Test the kind of promise you are holding.** Is it a personal assurance, a covenant command, or a statement about God's character? Knowing which changes how you carry it.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says this Word is settled forever and stands on it. Wisdom says that permanence belongs to what God actually said, so read carefully, in context, and do not manufacture a promise He did not make. Faith holds the sentence; wisdom reads the whole paragraph.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 24:32–35**, and note what Jesus attaches the statement to.
- **Read Isaiah 40:6–8 and 1 Peter 1:22–25 together**, and mark the shared line.
- **Write out one promise you have been carrying for years**, and the date you first received it.
- **Thank God for it as a present word**, not a past one.
