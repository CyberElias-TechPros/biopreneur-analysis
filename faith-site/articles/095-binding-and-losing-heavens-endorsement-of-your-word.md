Matthew 18 is a chapter about a brother who has sinned against you. It gives a process: go alone, then take two or three, then tell the church. And at the end of that very ordinary, uncomfortable process comes one of the largest promises in the New Testament.

"Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven" (Matthew 18:18). Here is the central truth of this teaching: **binding and loosing is not a spell but a verdict: heaven endorses the judgement of a church that has judged according to God's own mind.**

## Reading It in Its Setting

It would be easy to lift this verse into a standalone formula about commanding things. But Jesus places it at the end of a conversation about reconciliation, and the tenses matter.

The Greek construction is commonly read as a future perfect: whatever you bind on earth *shall have been bound* in heaven. The church is not initiating; it is ratifying. Heaven has already decided, and the church declares it.

**The Authority of the Believer** — the God-given right and ability, in Christ, to stand and act in His name — operates by agreement, not by decree-making. **The Kingdom of God** — His rule — is the government whose policy we announce. **Confession of the Word** — speaking in agreement with God — is how that announcement is made.

Think of a court clerk reading a judge's ruling. The clerk's voice carries weight because the decision was already made elsewhere.

### What "Bind" and "Loose" Meant

In first-century Jewish usage, the phrases referred to forbidding and permitting — declaring what was prohibited and what was allowed. They were rabbinic terms for authoritative interpretation.

Jesus gave that authority to a community of ordinary disciples, not to a panel of experts. He also framed it in chapter 16 with the image of keys and the promise that "the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18).

### What It Does Not Mean

It does not mean you can decide anything and heaven will comply. That would make God an endorser of your preferences rather than the Lord of your life.

It does not mean a believer never loses a battle, or that any loss proves a failure of technique. It also does not mean the authority can be exercised without relationship — the previous verse says, "For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them" (Matthew 18:20).

Have you ever treated authority as a way of getting God to agree with you, rather than as a way of agreeing with God? What might that reversal have cost you?

### The Two-Thirds Agreement

Notice the "two or three" condition. The authority of Matthew 18 is corporate before it is individual, and it is tied to the Name — gathered *in my name*, not merely gathered in a room.

Note where the two part company: Private certainty about a verdict produces isolation; shared agreement in the Name produces endorsement.

### Binding and Loosing in Practice

- **Binding a pattern of sin.** A church that names a destructive behaviour and refuses to bless it is exercising this authority.
- **Loosing a repentant person.** Paul instructed the Corinthians to forgive and comfort the man they had disciplined, "lest perhaps such a one should be swallowed up with overmuch sorrow" (2 Corinthians 2:7).
- **Binding a lie.** Declaring a doctrine or practice forbidden in the church.
- **Loosing the gospel's welcome.** Announcing that repentance is met with forgiveness.
- **In prayer.** Agreeing together about what is plainly God's will.

One more observation about the setting. The authority in Matthew 18 is granted in the middle of a conversation about a brother who has sinned, and Peter's very next question is, "how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him?" (Matthew 18:21). It is as though Jesus anticipated that the first use of church authority would be forgiveness, and the parable that follows — the unforgiving servant — makes the point unforgettable.

### The Verdict Is About a Person, Not a Preference

Paul applied this authority to a specific case and was explicit about its limits: "For I verily, as absent in body, but present in spirit, have judged already, as though I were present, concerning him that hath so done this deed, In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, when ye are gathered together, and my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 5:3–4).

Judged already, in the name, gathered together. Three conditions. And the purpose was restorative, not punitive — which is why the same apostle later insists the offender be forgiven and comforted (2 Corinthians 2:7). Authority that cannot restore is not kingdom authority.

## What This Means for You

The most important question is not whether you can bind something, but whether your judgement has been formed by God's word or by your preferences. Authority follows agreement.

**1. Learn God's verdicts before you pronounce yours.** Read before you speak.
**2. Seek agreement, not just consensus.** Two or three gathered in the Name, not merely in the habit.
**3. Never bind what God has loosed.** Legalism is a misuse of this authority.
**4. Never loose what God has bound.** Licence is the opposite misuse.
**5. Faith and wisdom handle verdicts with care.** Wisdom remembers that a human verdict has consequences for real people; authority is stewardship, not entertainment.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read Matthew 18:15–20 slowly** and mark each step.
- **Name one thing** in your own life you need to bind, and one you need to loose.
- **Pray with two others** this week, specifically agreeing together.
- **Examine one opinion** you have been asserting as though it were God's verdict.
