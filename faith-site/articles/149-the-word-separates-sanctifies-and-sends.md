John 17 is the prayer Jesus prayed the night before He died, and in the middle of it He asks for something for us.

"Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth" (John 17:17).

Then, without a pause: "As thou hast sent me into the world, even so have I also sent them into the world" (John 17:18).

Two movements in two verses. Set apart, and sent out.

Here is the central truth of this teaching: **sanctification is not withdrawal from the world but separation to a purpose within it — the Word cuts you loose from one allegiance and sends you into the place you were cut loose for.**

## The Word Behind "Sanctify"

The Greek is *hagiazō* — to make holy, to set apart, to consecrate. It is the same word family as *hagios*, holy.

And it is not primarily about being made morally flawless. It is about being designated, like a vessel in the temple set aside for one use, or a day set aside for one purpose.

**The Word of God** — what He has said — is the agent of that setting apart: "through thy truth." **The New Creation** — what you were made in Christ — is the thing being consecrated. **The Life of the Spirit** — His ongoing work in you — is the power that makes the consecration real.

Consider a surgeon's instruments. They are not "better" knives. They are set apart — cleaned, designated, used for one purpose, and never for anything else.

### Truth Is the Instrument, Not the Atmosphere

Note the mechanism: "through thy truth."

Not through experience, not through crisis, not through a committee. Through truth. And then the identification: "thy word is truth."

That is one of the highest things said about Scripture anywhere, and Jesus says it in a prayer rather than an argument. The Word is not merely true; it is the standard by which the word "true" has meaning.

Which means sanctification has content. You are not set apart by a feeling of devotion but by the truth taking hold of your thinking until your life reorders around it.

### What Sanctification Costs

The setting apart is not painless, and this is where honest teaching is needed.

To be set apart is to be separated from something. That is the cost, and it is why sanctification is often experienced as loss before it is experienced as liberty — the friendship that pulls you down, the habit that owns you, the ambition that has quietly become a master.

Set the two responses side by side. One believer treats every separation as a wound and resists it, and ends up with a life that belongs to everything. Another lets the Word do its cutting and discovers that what was removed was a weight rather than a limb.

### The Sending Is Not Optional

Here is the part of John 17 that gets forgotten in a great deal of teaching on holiness.

Jesus does not pray, "take them out of the world." He explicitly says the opposite a few verses earlier: "I pray not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil" (John 17:15).

And then He sends them — "as thou hast sent me." The pattern of the sending is His own incarnation: into the world, among people, at cost.

So sanctification that ends in withdrawal has misunderstood its own purpose. The set-apart life is set apart *for* the world it is sent into.

### The Pattern in the Epistles

Paul prays the same way, and always with the sending in view.

"That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word" (Ephesians 5:26) — and the goal is a church "without blemish" that is then presented.

And in Romans, the consecration of the body is followed immediately by the renewing of the mind, and then by a whole section on how to live with enemies, with authorities, with the weak, and with the neighbour. Set apart, then sent into ordinary relationships.

### The Order Matters

The sequence in John 17 is separation then sending, and the order is not accidental.

A person who is sent without being set apart carries his old allegiances with him and ends up serving two masters badly. A person who is set apart without being sent becomes a specialist in his own purity.

Both are failures, and both are common. The Word does the first so that the second is possible. Which failure is nearer to you?

### How It Happens in a Week

Practically, this is not a dramatic event but a slow transfer of allegiance.

A verse confronts a habit you have defended for years, and you let it go. A command you had been sidestepping becomes ordinary. A conversation you would have handled with your old temper is handled with something new. None of that is spectacular. All of it is sanctification.

And each of those small separations makes you more useful to the people you are sent to, because a person who is not owned by his appetites can be trusted with other people's needs.

## What This Means for You

**1. Expect both halves.** If your faith has become entirely about withdrawal, ask who you are being sent to. If it has become entirely about activity, ask what you are being separated from.

**2. Let one verse do its cutting.** Which truth have you been avoiding because obeying it would cost a separation?

**3. Do not confuse separation with superiority.** Set apart is a purpose, not a rank.

**4. Go where you are sent.** Your street, your workplace, your family. The sending is usually unglamorous.

**5. Let Faith and Wisdom work together here.** Faith says the Word sanctifies and sends. Wisdom says that being sent does not mean being reckless — that boundaries, rest, and the counsel of mature believers are part of how a set-apart person stays useful for decades rather than burning out in a year.

## Putting It to Work This Week

- **Read John 17:13–19** in one sitting.
- **Write one separation** the Word is asking of you, and one place you are being sent.
- **Read Ephesians 5:25–27**, and note the instrument and the goal.
- **Ask one person** how you could be useful to them this week.
